# Interruptibility, opportune moments and receptivity: when should a phone notification be delivered?

Reading notes for the report writer:
- "OLD" marks work published more than 10 years before November 2026 (i.e. before November 2016), with a note on whether it has been replicated.
- "Read in full text" means the numbers were taken from the paper itself. "Secondary" means the number comes from another paper's or article's summary and was not checked against the original.
- Percent improvements in this literature are almost always relative, not percentage points. Absolute values are given wherever the source provides them.

## 1. Breakpoint-timed delivery versus random or immediate delivery

### Takeaway
Delivering at a breakpoint (just after a call, a text, a sub-task or an app interaction) reliably makes people respond faster (roughly 25-50% shorter response times in five independent studies, from lab to a 680,000-user deployment) and lowers measured workload or frustration for a subset of users. It has much weaker and less consistent effects on whether people respond at all, and in the only mobile study that asked, people did not rate breakpoint timing as better. Every breakpoint detector in these studies relied on on-device signals (call/SMS events, UI events, accelerometer) that the companion app does not have.

### Cited Findings

**Desktop and lab origins (OLD)**
- Adamczyk and Bailey (CHI 2004, lab study, desktop editing/search/media tasks with a news-alert interruption): interruptions at predicted "best" points (task boundaries) "consistently produced less annoyance, frustration, and time pressure, required less mental effort" than at worst points. OLD; abstract-level only here, numbers not extracted. — [Adamczyk & Bailey 2004](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p271.pdf)
- Iqbal and Bailey's Oasis system (study first reported at CHI 2008, summarised in TOCHI 2010; 16 users doing programming and diagram-editing tasks, 16 notifications each): scheduling notifications at breakpoints "reduced frustration by 20% and reaction time by 25% relative to immediate delivery", at the cost of an average deferral of only about 90 seconds. OLD; direction replicated on mobile by Fischer 2011 and Okoshi 2015-2017. Read in full text. — [Iqbal & Bailey 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-TOCHI10.pdf)
- Same study: users wanted task-relevant notifications at fine/medium breakpoints while still in context, and general-interest notifications deferred to coarse breakpoints, "after they had left the main task and were more amenable to receiving general information". — [Iqbal & Bailey 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Iqbal-TOCHI10.pdf)
- Ho and Intille (CHI 2005, PDA plus two wireless accelerometers): interruptions delivered at a physical-activity transition (sit/stand/walk changes) "are judged more positively compared to interruptions delivered at random times". OLD; secondary (survey summary), sample size not extracted. — [Mehrotra & Musolesi survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)

**Fischer, Greenhalgh and Benford, MobileHCI 2011 (OLD; read in full text)**
- Design: 20 participants (10 male, 10 female, aged 21-48, half postgraduate students, UK), two weeks, 2,002 notifications, 1,380 completed (68.9% response). Notifications were sent either at a pseudo-random time or immediately after the participant ended a phone call or read an SMS; each asked for a multiple-choice answer, a free-text answer or a photo. — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Acceptance time (delivery to accepting the notification): median 36 s at random times versus 19 s after reading an SMS and 10 s after a call (differences significant at the .01 level). — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Total response time: median 66 s random versus 38 s after SMS and 29 s after a call (.01 level); SMS and call did not differ from each other. — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Non-response: 377 non-responses to random notifications versus 245 to opportune ones (chi-square(1) = 28.01, p < .001), but the overall association between timing and responding was weak (phi = .076). From the reported counts (723 responded + 377 not at random times; 657 + 245 at opportune times) the response rates work out to about 65.7% random versus 72.8% opportune; this percentage is my arithmetic, not a figure printed in the paper. — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Self-reports did not match behaviour: rated appropriateness of timing did not differ by condition (chi-square(2) = 5.65, p = .068) and neither did rated burden; in exit interviews participants disagreed substantially about which timing was best. The authors: hypotheses about behaviour "were supported", those about self-reports "were not supported". — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Participants' own explanation for the speed-up was that the phone was already in hand: "If I've already got it in my hand ... there's more chance of me responding to it." — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- What the notification asked for mattered about as much as when it arrived: median response time 29 s for multiple choice versus 53 s for free text and 48 s for photo; participants were 5.8% more likely to complete a multiple-choice task than a free-text one and 11.3% more likely than a photo task. NASA-TLX workload: free text 57.8, photo 40.3, multiple choice 31.0. — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)

**Okoshi et al., "Attelia" (breakpoints inside phone use, detected from UI events)**
- Controlled study, 37 participants: for the subgroup whose workload scores were sensitive to notification timing, breakpoint delivery gave a 46% lower NASA-TLX weighted workload than random timing. OLD (2015); read in full text. — [Okoshi et al. 2015/2016, Pervasive and Mobile Computing preprint](https://julian-ramos.github.io/papers/Okoshi2016.pdf)
- In-the-wild study, 30 participants (27 analysed), 16 days, daily NASA-TLX: for the 13 "sensitive" users mean workload was 34.22 with notifications disabled, 51.07 with random timing and 45.46 with breakpoint timing. The headline "33% lower cognitive load" is the reduction in the workload added by notifications (a 33% rise over baseline instead of 49%), not a 33% drop in the score. Random versus breakpoint p < .05. For the 14 "insensitive" users there were no significant differences between any strategies. — [Okoshi et al. 2015/2016](https://julian-ramos.github.io/papers/Okoshi2016.pdf)
- Same field study: response time to notifications was 13% quicker at breakpoints; the breakpoint classifier was 82.6% accurate. — [Okoshi et al. 2015/2016](https://julian-ramos.github.io/papers/Okoshi2016.pdf)
- A multi-device extension (UbiComp 2015) reported a 19.4% reduction in perceived workload on a smartwatch versus random delivery. OLD; search-snippet level, sample size not verified. — [Okoshi et al. UbiComp 2015](https://julian-ramos.github.io/papers/Okoshi2015.pdf)

**Yahoo! JAPAN production studies (the largest evidence on breakpoint timing)**
- Okoshi et al. (PerCom 2017): breakpoint-timed push in the Yahoo! JAPAN Android app, 687,840 users, 21 days. Breakpoints were detected from on-device activity recognition and phone events. User response time was "significantly reduced (49.7%)"; delivery was delayed by about 4 minutes on average. Secondary, from the authors' own follow-up paper and their research page. — [Tsubouchi & Okoshi 2017](https://interruptions.net/literature/Tsubouchi-UbiComp17.pdf); [LY Corporation Research](https://research.lycorp.co.jp/en/publications/239)
- Independent reading of the same paper: mean response time 27.32 minutes with breakpoint timing versus 54.30 minutes without, and "the breakpoint detection increased click-through rates and engagement scores, but the effect was not statistically significant." — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf); the authors' follow-up describes the same result more favourably as "higher click rate and user engagement level throughout the study period" — [Tsubouchi & Okoshi 2017](https://interruptions.net/literature/Tsubouchi-UbiComp17.pdf)
- Okoshi, Tsubouchi and Tokuda (KDD 2019): production deployment evaluated on 382,518 Yahoo! JAPAN Android users for 28 days, reported as a maximum 60.7% increase in click rate. Search-snippet level only; "maximum" implies a best-case segment and the average effect was not retrieved. — [Okoshi et al. 2019](https://researchgate.net/profile/Tadashi-Okoshi/publication/334712474_Real-World_Product_Deployment_of_Adaptive_Push_Notification_Scheduling_on_Smartphones/links/5d4a0d1aa6fdcc370a80ddee/Real-World-Product-Deployment-of-Adaptive-Push-Notification-Scheduling-on-Smartphones.pdf)

### Inferences
- The well-replicated effect of breakpoint timing is speed of response (five studies, three research groups, lab to production scale). The effect on whether a notification is acted on at all is small or statistically unclear in every study where it was tested separately (Fischer: weak association; Okoshi 2017: not significant per Pielot's reading; Okoshi 2019: positive but only a "maximum" figure found).
- Reduced annoyance or workload from breakpoint timing is supported in lab studies and in one small field study, and there only for roughly half the participants. In Fischer 2011 people did not perceive the opportune timing as better. So "less annoying" is less firmly established than "answered faster".
- All breakpoint detectors used signals the companion app lacks. The only breakpoint the app can observe directly is the end of the user's own exchange with it.
- For a system sending a few messages a week whose value does not depend on being answered within a minute, a halving of response latency (for example 54 to 27 minutes) may matter little; the literature does not test low-frequency, non-urgent nudges specifically.

### Gaps
- Exact click-through numbers from Okoshi et al. PerCom 2017 and the average (not maximum) effect in KDD 2019 could not be retrieved from primary text.
- No study was found testing breakpoint timing for notifications sent only a few times per week; all studies used several prompts per day.
- No study was found on breakpoints defined by a conversation with a chatbot ending.

## 2. What predicts receptivity, and how far do simple features get compared with rich sensing?

### Takeaway
Receptivity models built on phone sensors beat random timing by modest absolute margins (for example precision 7.1% versus 4.3%; response within 10 minutes 39% versus 28%), and the features that carry most of the signal are recency of phone interaction, lock/screen state, physical activity and, consistently but weakly, time of day and weekday/weekend. The single largest gain reported anywhere came not from sensors but from each user's own past response history (precision 21.8% versus 7.1% with 197 sensor features), which is available to any app. One small trial found sensor-driven timing no better than a fixed evening window.

### Cited Findings

**Baseline behaviour**
- Pielot, Church and de Oliveira (MobileHCI 2014; 15 users, one week, Spain; OLD): 63.5 notifications per day on average; median time to view ranged from 3.5 minutes (messengers, weekends) to 27.7 minutes (email, weekends); messengers 6.6 min on weekdays and 3.5 min on weekend days; the screen was off in 69.2% of cases when a notification arrived. Read in full text. — [Pielot et al. 2014](https://ic.unicamp.br/~oliveira/doc/MHCI2014_An-in-situ-study-of-mobile-phone-notifications.pdf)
- Dingler and Pielot (MobileHCI 2015; 42 users, two weeks, 55,000+ messages; OLD): people were attentive to messages about 12.1 hours a day, inattentive periods were often only 2-5 minutes long, and attentiveness could be predicted from phone usage with about 80% accuracy. Secondary. — [Mehrotra & Musolesi survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)
- Mehrotra et al. (CHI 2016): overall click rate for everyday notifications was 62.52% (10,372 notifications, 20 users). — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)

**Pielot et al., "Beyond Interruptibility" (IMWUT 2017; read in full text)**
- Design: 337 Android users recruited through an agency to match national age/gender distribution in a Western European country (age 18-66, mean 37.9), mean 27.4 active days, 78,930 questionnaire notifications, 120 million phone events. 38.9% of notifications were clicked; in 4.3% the participant went on to open suggested content (the target outcome). — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Model with all 197 sensor/usage features (XGBoost): precision 0.071 versus a 0.043 baseline (the "66.6% better" headline), recall 0.279, while sending only 16.7% of the notifications. — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Adding four features describing the user's own past responses raised precision to 0.218 (over 5 times baseline) and recall to 0.540, sending only 10.6% of notifications. The authors: this "does not require to capture mobile sensor data or any personal data". — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Engagement was highly concentrated: 80 of 337 participants never engaged with suggested content and 68 did so only once; roughly one third "were never or only once predicted to be open for engagement". — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Feature importance: hour of day was the second most important single feature (importance 0.0350, after minutes since the ringer mode was changed at 0.0365). But "conversion rates are largely the same, and only lower during the early hours of the day (7-8 am)"; "otherwise, we found that the time of the day had relatively little influence". — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Recent phone interaction: conversion was more likely the less time had passed since the last screen unlock (r = -0.069), last screen status change (r = -0.063), last app launch (r = -0.065) and last opening of the notification centre (r = -0.035), all p < .001. All individual correlations in the paper are small (|r| below 0.07). — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Other correlates: more likely at home or travelling than near work; more likely with fewer notifications received so far that day (r = -0.013) and less incoming communication; older participants more likely (r = 0.044). — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Cold start: with no history, building reliable past-behaviour features "could take weeks or months" depending on send frequency. — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)

**Künzler, Mishra et al. (IMWUT 2019; read in full text)**
- Design: 189 adults in German-speaking Switzerland (141 iOS, 48 Android; 119 female; median age 40), six weeks, physical-activity chatbot "Ally" whose conversations open with a generic greeting. 13,942 initiating messages. Responded at all: 72.0% iOS, 81.5% Android. Responded within 10 minutes ("just-in-time"): 21.2% iOS, 25.1% Android. Engaged in conversation within 10 minutes: 16.0% and 19.9%. — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)
- Phone interaction: on iOS an unlocked phone gave a significantly higher likelihood of just-in-time response (chi-square(1) = 19.115, p < .001) and conversation engagement (chi-square(1) = 15.570, p < .001). — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)
- Activity: response and engagement were significantly more likely when walking than when the phone was still (the authors' reading: a still phone is probably on a table, not on the person). — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)
- Person-level factors: just-in-time response rate rose with age (F(1,177) = 4.798, p = .029); gender had no effect; neuroticism raised just-in-time response; agreeableness, conscientiousness and neuroticism raised overall response rate. — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)
- Machine learning (10-fold cross-validation, iOS): contextual features alone gave F1 = 0.31 for just-in-time response, about 50% above a biased-random baseline; adding person-level factors gave F1 = 0.36 (71% above baseline). The abstract's "up to 77%" is the best case. Absolute F1 values are low. — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)
- People who met their step goals more often were more receptive (goal completion rate versus overall response rate r = 0.53, p < .001); direction of causation not established. — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)

**Mishra et al. (IMWUT 2021; read in full text): the only randomised live test of model-timed versus random-timed delivery found**
- Design: 83 iOS users recruited by Facebook ads (64 female; median age 30), three weeks each, 2,023 messages; each message was randomly assigned to be timed by a random-time control, a pre-trained static model or a per-user adaptive model. Model inputs: weekday/weekend, time of day (morning/afternoon/evening), battery status and level, lock state, time since lock change, Wi-Fi connection, physical activity. — [Mishra et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302)
- Response within 10 minutes: control 0.284; static model +0.108 (+38.0%, p < .001); adaptive model +0.044 (+15.5%, not significant, p = .27). — [Mishra et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302)
- Responding at all: control 0.656; static +0.080 (+12.2%, p = .003); adaptive +0.077 (+11.7%, p = .048). Conversation engagement: control 0.268; static +34% (p < .001); adaptive +12% (not significant). Response delay did not differ significantly. — [Mishra et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302)
- The abstract's "up to a 40% improvement" corresponds to the 38% best case; an earlier arXiv version quoted 36%. — [Mishra et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302); [arXiv listing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08302)
- Offline, the static model reached F1 = 0.36 versus 0.25 for a random baseline. — [Mishra et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302)

**Other models and trials**
- Mehrotra et al. (UbiComp 2015; 35 users, about 70,000 notifications, over three weeks; OLD): models using context plus the notification's type and sender relationship predicted acceptance with 70% sensitivity and 80% specificity, and "outperform subjective rules with which users describe their interruptibility". Secondary. — [Mehrotra & Musolesi survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)
- PrefMiner (Mehrotra et al. 2016; OLD): using only the user's location and the notification title, acceptance versus dismissal was predicted with 91% precision; in a live deployment 179 rules were suggested and 56.98% accepted by users. Secondary. — [Mehrotra & Musolesi survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)
- InterruptMe (Pejovic and Musolesi 2014; 20 participants, two weeks; OLD): opportune moments predicted with about 60% accuracy from location, activity, emotion and engagement. Secondary. — [Mehrotra & Musolesi survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)
- Morrison et al. (PLOS ONE 2017; stress-management app, 77 analysable participants of 162 randomised): sensor-driven "intelligent" timing (up to 3 per day) versus one notification per day at a random time in a fixed 17:00-20:00 window versus one every three days. The percentage of notifications viewed did not differ meaningfully between groups (eta-squared = .00); mean delay to viewing was about 163 minutes; the intelligent group had the highest two-week attrition (60% versus 42% daily and 39% occasional). The authors conclude that adaptive tailoring of timing "does not always enhance their use". Underpowered: all confidence intervals crossed zero. — [Morrison et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0169162)
- Sahu et al. (arXiv preprint, July 2025, not peer reviewed as retrieved; 70 university students, two weeks, 10,687 hourly prompts between 07:00 and 20:00, about 76% answered): completion was higher with the screen on (about 82%) or the phone unlocked (about 85%) and when walking (about 83%); a Thompson-sampling bandit was reported to raise average reward above baseline, with no numeric comparison retrievable. — [Sahu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02817v1)

**Recent LLM-era work (2024-2026)**
- Haag et al. (CHI 2025): GPT-4 generated just-in-time intervention decisions and messages for a conceptual cardiac-rehab scenario; 27 laypeople rated them above those written by laypeople and healthcare professionals. This evaluates message and decision quality on vignettes, not delivery timing or real-world response. — [Haag et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.08658)
- Jayasiriwardene et al. (OzCHI 2026, qualitative): a conversational agent elicits a user's routine and an LLM plus knowledge graph turns it into an intervention schedule. Reported problems: LLM extraction fragments people's non-linear accounts of their routines, and an "ecological mismatch" in which "algorithmic schedule availability often ignores users' fluctuating psychological receptivity and physical energy". No quantitative results in the abstract. — [Jayasiriwardene et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09294)

### Inferences
- Of the feature families that carry signal, a sensor-less app can obtain: time of day, weekday/weekend, the user's own response history, and "recently interacted" only in the narrow form of the user having just messaged the app. It cannot obtain lock state, physical activity, location or general phone use, which are the strongest in-the-moment context signals.
- The most transferable quantitative result is Pielot 2017's: response history outperformed 197 sensor features by a factor of about three in precision. This comes from one study with an unusual outcome (clicking suggested content inside a survey), so the size of the gain should not be assumed to carry over.
- Mishra 2021's model used only coarse time features plus phone-state features and gained about 11 percentage points in 10-minute response and 8 points in any response over random timing. The paper does not report a time-features-only ablation, so the share attributable to time of day and weekday alone is unknown.
- A stated routine (work hours, sleep hours) is a plausible stand-in for the "at work versus at home" and "phone not on person" effects, but Fischer 2010 (section 3) found that the times people nominate in advance as good were no better than other times, and the 2026 OzCHI study reports a similar schedule-versus-receptivity mismatch. Stated routine is better supported as a way to exclude bad windows than to pick good moments.
- Absolute predictive performance is low everywhere (F1 0.3-0.4; precision 7%). Even with full sensing, timing models shift odds; they do not identify moments with confidence.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares a time-of-day/day-of-week-only model against a full sensor model on the same data with reported numbers. Mishra 2021 and Künzler 2019 include time features but report no such ablation.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that uses an LLM to predict receptivity or choose delivery time and evaluates it against real response data. Work found is vignette-based or qualitative.
- The Künzler, Kramer and Kowatsch 2017 meta-analysis of context-aware notification management systems (IEEE WiMob) could not be retrieved; its pooled effect sizes are not reported here.
- The original Mehrotra 2015, PrefMiner, Dingler 2015 and Sahami Shirazi 2014 papers were not read in full; their numbers are from the 2017/2018 survey or abstracts.

## 3. How much do content and sender matter relative to timing?

### Takeaway
In the studies that compare them directly, what the notification says and who it is from explain more of whether it is welcomed and accepted than when it arrives; timing mainly affects how quickly it is noticed. The direct head-to-head experiment is old and tiny (11 co-workers), but the pattern is consistent with two later in-the-wild studies and with users' stated reasons for clicking and dismissing.

### Cited Findings
- Fischer et al. (MobileHCI 2010; OLD; read in full text): 11 co-workers (PARC), 10 days, 641 SMS sent, 418 responses (65.2%). Content from categories the participant had rated interesting versus uninteresting was crossed with delivery at self-chosen "good times" versus other times. Content had a large effect on gut reaction (F(1,10) = 30.95, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .76; mean 3.96 versus 2.86); time of delivery had none (F(1,10) = 2.8, p = .125) and there was no interaction. — [Fischer et al. 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI10.pdf)
- Same study: neither content nor delivery time significantly affected perceived timeliness. 8 of 11 participants said content mattered more than timing; one put it as "When the content was bad, I felt annoyed, when the timing was bad, I just let it go." 9 disliked the content of most messages versus 5 who said most arrived at a bad time. — [Fischer et al. 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI10.pdf)
- The authors' own caveat: the null timing result "suggests that people are bad at predicting the times at which they will be receptive to messages and shows that time of delivery is an incomplete operationalisation of timing – not that timing doesn't matter." Interest, entertainment, relevance and actionability of content each significantly raised receptivity. — [Fischer et al. 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI10.pdf)
- Mehrotra et al. (CHI 2016; OLD by a few months; read in full text): 74 installs via Google Play without payment, about two months, analysis restricted to 20 users (11 male, 9 female, aged 19-50) who answered at least 14 questionnaires: 10,372 notifications and 474 questionnaire responses. — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Reasons given for clicking (multiple allowed): "At this moment, I was free" 37.2%; sender is important 31.5%; content is useful 31.5%; content is important 27.1%; action does not require a lot of effort 20.2%; was waiting for it 15.8%; content is urgent 14.5%. — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Reasons given for dismissing: content not urgent, not important or not useful were each chosen in roughly 38-43% of cases; sender not important 19.6%; "I was busy" 19.6%; too much effort 3.6%. The authors: users "very rarely state that they were busy and thus had to dismiss a notification". — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Perceived disruption lowers acceptance but explains little of it: odds of acceptance fall by a factor of 0.581 (95% CI 0.497-0.675) per point of disruption on a 5-point scale, with R-squared = 0.1434. 104 of 474 notifications were rated disruptive and 54% of those were still accepted, mainly because the content was useful (35.2%) or important (33.3%). — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Sender: decision time was fastest for a partner (mean 3.3 s) and immediate family (4.9 s). Perceived disruption was highest when the sender "is not a person" (system/app messages) or is a work subordinate, and lowest for extended family (F(10,444) = 3.987, p < .001). — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- What timing/context does affect is noticing: time until a notification was seen averaged 47 s while the user was communicating versus 9 min 30 s while idle (5 min 45 s otherwise), and 7.3 min in silent mode versus 3 min 21 s on vibrate. Perceived disruption rose with task complexity (correlation 0.477, p < .001) and was highest mid-task, lowest when idle or just starting a task. — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- The survey's synthesis: attentiveness is driven by ringer mode, presentation and task engagement, whereas "receptivity to notifications is influenced by the type of information contained in the notification and the relationship between the sender and the recipient"; "the information triggered by proactive services are considered as least interesting by the users". — [Mehrotra & Musolesi survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)
- Sahami Shirazi et al. (CHI 2014; OLD): close to 200 million notifications from more than 40,000 users; focus on which notification types users value. Detailed figures not retrieved. — [Sahami Shirazi et al. 2014](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2556288.2557189)
- Required effort is part of "content": in Fischer 2011 a one-tap multiple-choice response was completed in a median 29 s versus 53 s for free text and was 5.8% more likely to be completed. — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Pielot 2017's limitation statement makes the same point from the other side: their model predicts timing only, and "whether users finally engage with an app should depend on many more factors than the timing of the engagement notification". — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)

### Inferences
- The evidence separates two outcomes: timing and alert modality govern how fast a notification is seen; content value, sender and effort govern whether it is accepted and how it is felt. A nudge about something the user personally asked to be reminded of sits on the favourable side of every content factor these studies identify (relevance, interest, actionability), but none of the studies tested self-authored reminders.
- The companion app is a non-person, proactive sender, which is the category Mehrotra 2016 found most disruptive and the survey calls least interesting. Whether a conversational agent the user has a history with is perceived like a "system" sender or like a contact is not addressed by this literature.
- Action buttons correspond to the low-effort response format that was fastest and most completed in Fischer 2011.

### Gaps
- No study was found that estimates, in one design with adequate power, the relative share of variance in response explained by content versus timing. Fischer 2010 (n = 11) is the only direct factorial test.
- No evidence was found on notifications whose content is the user's own previously stated intention.

## 4. Is a moment when the user is already on the phone or in the app better than a cold push?

### Takeaway
Yes for speed and probability of response, on consistent evidence from at least five independent studies: an unlocked or just-used phone, or the end of a communication episode, goes with faster and more frequent responses. It is not established that such moments feel better to the user, and no study was found on delivering a proactive message immediately after the user finishes talking to a chatbot.

### Cited Findings
- After a call or SMS versus random: median acceptance time 10-19 s versus 36 s; fewer non-responses; no difference in rated appropriateness. (20 participants; OLD.) — [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Unlocked phone: significantly higher likelihood of responding within 10 minutes and of engaging in conversation (iOS, 141 users). — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)
- Less time since last unlock, screen change and app launch each correlated with higher engagement (r between -0.063 and -0.069, p < .001; 337 users). — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Screen on or unlocked: prompt completion about 82% and 85% (70 students; preprint). — [Sahu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02817v1)
- While the user was communicating, notifications were seen in 47 s on average versus 9.5 min when idle; yet perceived disruption was highest in the middle of a task and lowest when idle or starting one. — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- Breakpoints within phone use (Attelia and the Yahoo! JAPAN deployment) roughly halved response time at scale, with unclear click-through effects. — [Tsubouchi & Okoshi 2017](https://interruptions.net/literature/Tsubouchi-UbiComp17.pdf); [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- A cold push often has to pull the user to the phone: in 69.2% of arrivals the screen was off, and the authors infer that "notifications often triggered interaction with the phone". — [Pielot et al. 2014](https://ic.unicamp.br/~oliveira/doc/MHCI2014_An-in-situ-study-of-mobile-phone-notifications.pdf)
- Cold pushes at a fixed time still work as prompts: in the Drink Less micro-randomised trial (349 users, 30 days) an 8 pm notification raised the probability of opening the app in the next hour 3.5-fold (relative risk 3.52, 95% CI 2.92-4.26; observed proportions about 0.12-0.13 with a notification versus 0.036 without). — [Bell et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- Vendor figures on in-app messages versus push are inconsistent and not comparable: Airship claims message-centre messages get "8x the direct response rates of push" with a 26% average engagement rate, while other vendor blogs surfaced in search quote figures pointing in both directions (for example in-app inbox open rates above 22% versus about 5% for push in one, and push click-through of 28% versus 7.4% for in-app in another; these were seen only as search summaries and their attribution to a specific vendor was not verified). All are marketing sources with undefined denominators (in-app messages are only shown to people who already opened the app). — [Airship](https://www.airship.com/?p=856)

### Inferences
- "User just messaged the app" is the one signal the companion app has that maps onto the best-supported predictor family (phone in hand, unlocked, end of a communication episode). By analogy with Fischer 2011, a nudge appended shortly after an exchange ends should be seen within seconds. This is an extrapolation: Fischer tested the end of calls and SMS with other people, not the end of a chatbot conversation.
- Mehrotra 2016 implies a trade-off the companion app cannot resolve without sensing: moments of active phone use give the fastest noticing but, if the user is mid-task, the highest perceived disruption; idle moments give the lowest disruption but slow noticing.
- Restricting nudges to moments of user-initiated contact would tie reach to how often the user opens the conversation; Pielot 2017's recall figures (27.9% of opportune moments caught) illustrate the general cost of waiting for a good moment, and they recommend a timeout after which the notification is sent anyway or dropped.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found comparing an in-conversation or just-after-conversation proactive message with a cold push for a chatbot or assistant.
- No independent (non-vendor) dataset comparing in-app message and push response rates was found.

## 5. Time-of-day and day-of-week patterns

### Takeaway
Time-of-day effects exist but are modest, differ between studies and populations, and the only consistent finding is negative: early morning (roughly before 8-10 am) is worst in every study that reports it. Beyond that, results conflict (daytime best in one Swiss study, evening or midday best in others, no effect in a 126,000-user advertising study), and day-of-week findings disagree in both academic and industry data.

### Cited Findings

**Randomised evidence**
- Bidargaddi et al. (JMIR mHealth 2018; micro-randomised trial, 1,255 users of a workplace wellbeing app, 89 days, six candidate send times per day: 8:30, 12:30, 17:30, 18:30, 19:30, 20:30): a push raised the chance of using the app in the next 24 hours by 3.9% relative (risk ratio 1.039, 95% CI 1.01-1.08). Weekend effect 8.7% (95% CI 1.01-1.17) versus weekday 2.5% (CI includes 1), but the weekday/weekend difference was not significant (p = .18). Exploratory, untested: largest effects at 12:30 pm (8.8% overall, 11.8% on weekends). The paper's abstract and results give different confidence intervals for the weekend-midday figure. — [Bidargaddi et al. 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123/)
- Bell et al. (JMIR mHealth 2023; Drink Less alcohol app, UK, 566 analysed users, 30 days): within the 349-user micro-randomised arm, an 8 pm notification gave relative risk 3.52 (95% CI 2.92-4.26) for opening the app between 8 and 9 pm, and 1.26 (1.19-1.34) over 24 hours. Parallel arms: daily 11 am standard notification (n = 120) versus randomised 8 pm (n = 349) versus none (n = 97): median sessions over 30 days 10.5, 9 and 6; median time to disengagement 11, 11 and 7 days (log-rank p = .42, underpowered). 11 am and 8 pm were not shown to differ. — [Bell et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)

**Observational evidence from receptivity studies**
- Künzler et al. 2019 (messages sent in three windows: before 10 am, 10 am-6 pm, after 6 pm): iOS users were most likely to respond within 10 minutes between 10 am and 6 pm and least likely after 6 pm (chi-square(2) = 47.237, p < .001); more likely on weekdays than weekends (chi-square(1) = 7.853, p < .005); least likely on weekend mornings before 10 am; on weekends conversation engagement was highest at night. Android users (n = 48) showed a similar daytime peak and morning low, with a non-significant tendency to respond more on weekend daytimes, the opposite of iOS; the paper's Android statistics are internally inconsistent (described as significant but reported as p = 0.223). — [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)
- Pielot et al. 2017: conversion "largely the same" across the day and "only lower during the early hours of the day (7-8 am)"; later in the day slightly better (r = 0.011); more engagement at home or travelling than near work. — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Westermann et al. (CHI EA 2016), as summarised by Pielot et al.: in a study with 126,000 users of a shopping-brochure app, "solely relying on the time of the day for sending notifications with recommendations had virtually no impact on how fast people engaged". Pielot et al.'s overall reading of the literature: "there is no conclusive evidence that engaging users during certain hours of the day is a promising strategy." Secondary. — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Sahu et al. 2025 (preprint; students; prompts 07:00-20:00): completion lower and responses slower in the morning than afternoon or evening (p < .001); afternoon and evening did not differ; weekends had lower completion than weekdays (p = .004), though the omnibus day-of-week test was not significant (p = .052). — [Sahu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.02817v1)
- Pielot et al. 2014 (OLD): 78.4% of notifications arrived on weekdays (fewer per day at weekends); messenger notifications were viewed faster on weekend days (3.5 versus 6.6 min) and email slower (27.7 min). — [Pielot et al. 2014](https://ic.unicamp.br/~oliveira/doc/MHCI2014_An-in-situ-study-of-mobile-phone-notifications.pdf)
- People in several studies show more stimulation-seeking phone use in the evenings (boredom, engagement and ritualistic-use studies, as summarised by Pielot et al.). — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Self-chosen times: in Fischer 2010 the three daily times each participant nominated as good produced no better reaction or perceived timeliness than other times. (n = 11; OLD.) — [Fischer et al. 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI10.pdf)

**Industry datasets (low reliability: vendor or third-party summaries, marketing pushes, undefined methods)**
- Batch 2025 benchmark (described as 800 billion messages), as summarised by a third party: open rates are fairly flat at roughly 4.5-5.1% for sends between 8 am and 7 pm, then decline to about 3.2% at 11 pm. — [MobiLoud summary of Batch data](https://www.mobiloud.com/blog/best-time-to-send-push-notifications)
- Day of week conflicts across sources: Tapjoy (2016, 4.4 million pushes; OLD) had Monday highest at 3.06% and Saturday lowest at 2.60%; summaries of Batch name Monday strongest; other roundups name Tuesday highest and Friday or Thursday lowest. — [GamesBeat on Tapjoy](https://gamesbeat.com/tapjoy-unveils-7-surprising-facts-about-who-opens-push-notifications/); [MobiLoud](https://www.mobiloud.com/blog/best-time-to-send-push-notifications); [Messageflow](https://messageflow.com/?p=2690)
- Braze reports an overall push total open rate of 3.96%, a reminder of how low marketing-push baselines are compared with the 60-80% response rates in research studies of personally relevant prompts. — [Braze benchmarks](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/benchmarks.md)

### Inferences
- The defensible general rule from this evidence is exclusionary: avoid early morning (and, trivially, sleep hours); between mid-morning and evening the differences are small relative to between-person differences and reverse between studies.
- Reported "best" windows track the population's routine (Swiss working adults: weekday daytime; students: afternoon/evening; workplace-wellbeing users: weekend midday), which supports anchoring to the individual's stated routine and then learning from that individual's own response history, not adopting a universal best hour. This is an inference; no study tested routine-anchored timing against a fixed clock time.
- Weekday versus weekend effects are inconsistent in direction (Künzler iOS: weekdays better; Künzler Android and Bidargaddi: weekends nominally better; Sahu: weekdays better), so no day-type preference is established.
- Industry open-rate curves describe bulk marketing pushes and mostly reflect when people are awake and using phones; they add little beyond "daytime is flat, late night is lower".

### Gaps
- No randomised trial was found that compares several fixed clock times head to head with adequate power for a personal-reminder style message. Bidargaddi's per-slot results are exploratory and Bell's 11 am versus 8 pm comparison is underpowered.
- The original Westermann 2016 paper and the original Batch and Airship reports were not read; figures are second-hand.
- No evidence was found on time-of-day effects for messages sent only a few times a week.

## 6. Boredom and idle time as an opportune moment

### Takeaway
One well-known study shows boredom can be inferred from phone-usage logs and that people inferred to be bored were more likely to open suggested entertainment content, but it is old, the behavioural test had 16 participants, and it depends entirely on phone-usage sensing. Related evidence is mixed on whether idle moments suit a nudge: disruption is lowest when idle, but notifications are noticed slowest then.

### Cited Findings
- Pielot, Dingler, San Pedro and Oliver (UbiComp 2015; OLD): 54 participants, two weeks, over 40 million usage logs and 4,398 boredom self-reports; a user-independent model inferred boredom with up to 82.9% AUCROC. Predictive features included time since the last call or text, time of day and intensity of phone use. Secondary (press summary and authors' slides; paper PDF not retrievable). — [TechXplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2015-09-user-consume-content.html); [authors' slides](https://de.slideshare.net/mpielot/when-attention-is-not-scarce-detecting-boredom-from-mobile-phone-usage)
- Follow-up field test in the same paper (16 participants): people the model identified as bored were more likely to click a suggested BuzzFeed article and to stay on it at least 30 seconds than when notified at other times. Exact click and engagement ratios could not be verified from an accessible source. — [TechXplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2015-09-user-consume-content.html)
- Pielot et al. describe the result as specific to "a specific type of content, namely entertaining news articles", and note that people high in boredom-proneness were more likely to click article suggestions. — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Consistent signal in a later, larger study: openness to suggested content went with less incoming communication, which the authors link to boredom "–a state with desires for stimuli–". — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Idle is not the same as attentive: self-reported idle moments had the lowest perceived disruption but the longest time-to-seen (9 min 30 s versus 47 s when communicating). The authors: users "might be willing to accept more notifications when idle" but take longer to attend to them. — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)
- The most common stated reason for clicking a notification was "At this moment, I was free" (37.2%). — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)

### Inferences
- Boredom detection as published cannot be reproduced by an app without phone-usage logs. The only overlapping input is time of day (evening stimulation-seeking), which is weak evidence by itself.
- The boredom result concerns appetite for entertainment content. Whether a bored moment is good for a prompt to do an errand or plan a trip was not tested; ideas and trip-planning nudges are arguably closer to "stimulation" than errands are, but that is speculation beyond the sources.

### Gaps
- No replication of the boredom-triggered delivery experiment was found, and the original effect sizes for the 16-person test could not be verified.
- No study was found linking boredom or idle time to acting on task or errand reminders.

## 7. Known limitations of this evidence base

### Takeaway
The field rests mostly on small, short, self-selected, sensor-rich studies in Western Europe, the US and Japan, many evaluated offline and several now more than ten years old; outcomes are usually response speed or clicks within minutes, not whether the user was glad to be nudged or acted on the content weeks later. Almost nothing addresses low-frequency, personally authored reminders from a sensor-less conversational agent.

### Cited Findings
- The field's own survey: "Almost all studies in the area of interruptibility management are conducted with small samples of the population and for short time periods", recruited through researchers' networks with self-selection bias, and validated "in an offline fashion ... the results presented in these studies do not have ecological validity". — [Mehrotra & Musolesi survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)
- Sample sizes and durations of the core studies: Fischer 2010 n = 11 co-workers, 10 days; Fischer 2011 n = 20, two weeks; Pielot 2014 n = 15, one week; Attelia field n = 30, 16 days; Mehrotra 2016 n = 20 analysed of 74, about two months; boredom n = 54 and n = 16, two weeks; Morrison 2017 n = 77 analysed; Mishra 2021 n = 83, three weeks; Künzler 2019 n = 189, six weeks; Pielot 2017 n = 337, about four weeks. Only the Yahoo! JAPAN deployments (687,840 and 382,518 users, 21-28 days), Bidargaddi 2018 (1,255 users, 89 days) and Bell 2023 (566 users, 30 days) are larger. — sources as cited in sections 1-6
- Attrition and selection: Mehrotra 2016 kept 20 of 74 installers ("Many users stopped responding to the questionnaires after a few days"); Künzler 2019 analysed only participants above a response-count threshold; Morrison 2017 lost 53% of users within two weeks and analysed 77 of 162 randomised. — [Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf); [Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf); [Morrison et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0169162)
- Incentives distort response: Pielot 2017 participants were incentivised to answer a minimum number of notifications, "which in a real context would not be the case", and the notification did not reveal its content, so findings "may therefore not generalize" to notifications that show their content. — [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)
- Novelty and decay: in Mishra 2021 the 10-minute response rate to randomly timed messages "dropped significantly" over three weeks (p = .011) and conversation engagement also declined (p = .005), while the adaptive model trended upward (not significant for response, p = .287). In Bidargaddi 2018 the notification effect attenuated over 89 days but not significantly (p = .84). In Bell 2023 no significant change was detected over 30 days (0.993 per day, 95% CI 0.975-1.012). — [Mishra et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302); [Bidargaddi et al. 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123/); [Bell et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- Heterogeneity between people: Attelia's workload benefit appeared only in the timing-sensitive cluster (13 of 27 users); in Pielot 2017, 80 of 337 never engaged and 68 only once; Fischer 2011 interviewees disagreed on which timing was best. — [Okoshi et al. 2015/2016](https://julian-ramos.github.io/papers/Okoshi2016.pdf); [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf); [Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)
- Platform and population limits: Pielot 2017 Android only, one Western European country; Künzler 2019 German-speaking Swiss insurance customers; Mishra 2021 iOS only, 77% female, recruited through Facebook ads, with a bug that kept the adaptive model from triggering for the first weeks of data collection; Sahu 2025 university students; Okoshi studies a single Japanese news/portal app. iOS and Android users behaved differently in Künzler 2019, which the authors attribute partly to how each OS groups notifications. — sources as cited above
- Lab versus field: the cleanest annoyance and frustration effects (Adamczyk and Bailey; Iqbal and Bailey; Attelia lab, 46%) come from controlled desktop or lab tasks; the field effect in Attelia was smaller (workload added by notifications cut by a third, in sensitive users only) and Fischer 2011's field study found no self-reported benefit. — sources as cited in section 1
- Underpowered or internally inconsistent reporting: Morrison 2017 (all confidence intervals crossed zero); Bell 2023 secondary analyses (566 recruited versus 2,000 planned, during the first UK COVID-19 lockdown); Bidargaddi 2018 (abstract and results give different intervals; discussion says the effect was "sustained" while results describe attenuation); Künzler 2019 Android time-of-day statistics. — sources as cited above
- Surveys available for further background: Mehrotra and Musolesi (2017/2018) on intelligent notification systems; Anderson et al. (IMWUT 2018) on attention management systems, which names Turner et al.'s interruptibility-prediction survey as the closest related review. — [Mehrotra & Musolesi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10171); [Anderson et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.06771)

### Inferences
- Age of evidence: the breakpoint studies with the cleanest designs (2004-2011) and the content-versus-timing experiment (2010) predate modern notification handling (grouped notifications, scheduled summaries, focus modes, action buttons on the lock screen). Their direction has been replicated (speed of response) or echoed (content dominance) in 2016-2021 work, but their exact magnitudes should not be assumed to hold in 2026.
- Outcome mismatch: most studies measure response within minutes. For a companion app the relevant outcomes are closer to "acted on within a day", "not annoyed" and "did not mute the app", which only Bidargaddi (24-hour engagement), Bell (disengagement) and Morrison (attrition) approximate, none with strong conclusions.
- Dose mismatch: study protocols send 2-14 prompts per day; evidence on whether timing matters more or less at a few messages per week is absent, and decay findings from high-frequency protocols may overstate fatigue at low frequency or understate it over months.
- No evidence was located from non-WEIRD populations other than one Indian student preprint and the Japanese deployments, so cultural generalisability (for example to different work-hour and messaging norms) is untested.

### Gaps
- Long-term (multi-month) studies of receptivity to low-frequency proactive messages were not found.
- Turner et al.'s interruptibility-prediction survey and the full text of Anderson et al. 2018 were not read; any additional limitations they document are not captured here.
- No 2022-2026 large-scale replication of the Pielot 2017 or Mishra 2021 results was found in this search; absence here reflects a limited search, not confirmed absence in the literature.
