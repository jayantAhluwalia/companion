# Intention–action behavioural science: evidence for timing, wording and structure of follow-through nudges

Provenance tags used on every finding (read these before quoting a number):
**[V]** = confirmed this session against the paper's abstract, full text or bibliographic record. **[S]** = secondary source only (press release, blog, news summary); primary not opened. **[R]** = figure recalled from the researcher's prior knowledge of the paper; a DOI link is supplied but the page was not opened this session, so check the exact number before quoting it. Research was done November 2026.

## 1. Intention–behaviour gap: how large is it?

### Takeaway
Intentions are the best single predictor of behaviour but leave most of it unexplained: people act on roughly half of their intentions, and experimentally strengthening an intention by a medium-to-large amount moves behaviour only by a small-to-medium amount. The gap is mostly "intended but did not act", which is exactly the population a self-captured-intention app serves.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of meta-analyses (422 studies, N = 82,107): intention–behaviour r = .53, i.e. intentions explain about 28% of variance in behaviour (correlational). **[R]** — [Sheeran 2002, Eur. Rev. Soc. Psychol.](https://doi.org/10.1080/14792772143000003)
- The gap is driven mainly by "inclined abstainers": a median of about 47% of people with positive intentions failed to act across the health behaviours decomposed. **[R]** — [Sheeran 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/14792772143000003)
- Meta-analysis of 47 experiments that successfully changed intentions: a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) produced a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36). Effects were weaker when behaviour was habitual or under low personal control. **[R]** — [Webb & Sheeran 2006, Psychol. Bull.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.2.249)
- Review conclusion: "intentions get translated into action approximately one-half of the time"; failures are grouped into failing to get started, failing to keep going, and failing to close/disengage. **[R]** — [Sheeran & Webb 2016, Soc. Personal. Psychol. Compass](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12265)

### Inferences
- The app should not spend its messages on persuading (raising motivation); the user already stated the intention. The leverage is in the "getting started" problems the literature names: forgetting, missing the opportune moment, and not knowing the first step.
- A realistic ceiling: even well-designed prompts will convert a minority of dormant intentions. Success metrics should be set in single-digit to low-double-digit percentage-point lifts, not "most notes get done".

### Gaps
- These estimates come largely from health behaviours and student samples; no source found quantifies the gap for casual, low-stakes personal intentions (errands, hobby projects, trips).
- Exact figures above are recalled, not re-opened this session.

## 2. Implementation intentions and planning prompts ("if X, then I will Y")

### Takeaway
If–then planning is one of the best-supported self-regulation tools, but the headline d ≈ 0.65 is a lab-heavy, uncorrected figure; field planning prompts typically add 1–4 percentage points for one-off actions, and a well-powered field test on a repeated behaviour (gym) found a precise null. Plans supplied by an experimenter worked as well as self-written ones in the one direct test found.

### Cited Findings
- Original meta-analysis: 94 independent tests, about 8,000 participants, d = 0.65 on goal attainment; mostly lab and student studies. **[R]** — [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Adv. Exp. Soc. Psychol.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)
- Updated meta-analysis of 642 independent tests: implementation intentions were effective for cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes, with effects ranging .27 ≤ d ≤ .66 across outcome types and study characteristics. **[V]** (abstract via journal record) — [Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024, Eur. Rev. Soc. Psychol.](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)
- Same meta-analysis, moderators: effects were larger when the plan was in if–then (contingent) format, when the person was highly motivated toward the goal, and when the plan was rehearsed. **[S]** (secondary summary; matches researcher's recollection of the abstract) — [goalsandprogress.com summary](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-research/)
- Domain meta-analyses give smaller field-relevant effects: physical activity SMD ≈ 0.31 post-intervention and ≈ 0.24 at follow-up **[R]** — [Bélanger-Gravel et al. 2013, Health Psychol. Rev.](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2011.560095); eating: d ≈ 0.51 for adding healthy foods but ≈ 0.29 for cutting unhealthy ones **[R]** — [Adriaanse et al. 2011, Appetite](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2010.10.012)
- Narrative review of mechanisms: if–then plans work by heightening cue accessibility and automating the response; they presuppose an existing goal commitment. **[R]** — [Bieleke, Keller & Gollwitzer 2021, Eur. Rev. Soc. Psychol.](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2020.1808936)
- Field, flu shots (N = 3,272 employees): a mailer prompting people to write the date and time they would get vaccinated raised vaccination from 33.1% to 37.1% (+4.2 pp); a date-only prompt gave a smaller, non-significant +1.5 pp. **[R]** — [Milkman et al. 2011, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1103170108)
- Field, voting (N = 287,228): a phone script asking when, where and how people would vote raised turnout 4.1 pp among those reached, and 9.1 pp in single-eligible-voter households; a standard get-out-the-vote call had little effect. **[R]** — [Nickerson & Rogers 2010, Psychol. Sci.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797609359326)
- Field, colonoscopy: a planning prompt on a reminder mailer raised screening from 6.2% to 7.2% (+1.0 pp). **[R]** — [Milkman et al. 2013, Prev. Med.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ypmed.2012.10.021)
- Review of 60+ papers: planning prompts help most when people already hold a strong intention, when the action can be completed in one go, and when there are obstacles or a risk of forgetting; plans help people "recall in the right circumstances and in the right moment"; prompts preserve autonomy because they do not tell people what to do. **[V/S]** (PDF located; conditions list from secondary coverage) — [Rogers, Milkman, John & Norton 2015, Behav. Sci. & Policy](https://behavioralpolicy.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/BSP_vol1is2_-Rogers.pdf)
- Failed field test: 877 gym members randomised to schedule the days/times of gym visits for two weeks vs. a control; "tightly estimated null" on attendance from check-in records, even though people did make plans and the plans predicted when they attended. Authors suggest repeated behaviours are harder to shift with simple planning than one-time actions. **[V]** — [Carrera, Royer, Stehr, Sydnor & Taubinsky 2018, NBER w24959 / J. Health Econ.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24959)
- System-supplied vs self-written plans: in an RCT (N = 248, alcohol), choosing an experimenter-provided implementation intention was as effective as writing one's own (difference p = .62); self-generated plans worked only for those who complied with the instruction. **[V]** (abstract-level) — [Armitage 2009, Health Psychol. (record)](https://peeref.com/works/10020182)
- Possible cost of specificity: the same lab that showed plans quiet the mind also published "Committed but close-minded: when making a specific plan for a goal hinders success" (specific plans can reduce use of unplanned opportunities). **[V]** (title/abstract-level only) — [Masicampo publications list](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/publications.html)

### Inferences
- "Next steps" should be phrased as a cue-linked plan, not a to-do: "When you're next at the pharmacy, pick up beard oil" rather than "Buy beard oil". The if–then format and a concrete situational cue are the active ingredients identified by the moderators.
- It is reasonable for the app to draft the plan and let the user accept or edit it with one tap: the only direct test found (Armitage 2009) shows provided plans work as well as self-written ones, and field prompts (Milkman 2011) were pre-structured by the sender. This rests on one study in one domain, so treat it as supported but not settled.
- Ask for a time and place, not just a day: date-plus-time beat date-only in the flu study.
- Expect planning to help one-off, single-session actions (service the bike, book the trip) more than recurring ones (exercise habit). The Carrera null is the relevant warning for recurring intentions.
- Plans only help when the intention is still live; a planning prompt on a stale idea ("maybe build a wardrobe light") is predicted to do little. Check commitment first ("Still want to do this?") before proposing a plan.
- Keep an escape hatch in plans for opportunistic completion (e.g. "or sooner if you pass a shop"), given the "close-minded" finding.

### Gaps
- Bias-corrected estimates for the 2024 meta-analysis could not be confirmed. A search summary reported trim-and-fill d ≈ 0.35 and a robust-Bayesian d ≈ 0.15 for this literature, but no primary source was reached (publisher returned HTTP 403); do not quote these figures without checking the paper.
- No study found tests implementation intentions generated by an AI/LLM system for a user's own free-text goals.
- Bieleke et al. 2021 was not opened; no large pre-registered multi-lab replication of the basic implementation-intention effect was found in this search.

## 3. Reminders: effect sizes, scale effects and message features

### Takeaway
Reminders reliably work, but at scale the lift is small in absolute terms: typically 1–5 percentage points for one-off actions, versus much larger effects in small published studies. The features with the best megastudy support are (a) framing the thing as already reserved/waiting for the person, (b) a second reminder a few days later, and (c) sending close to the moment the action can be taken; clever psychological content beyond a plain reminder added little.

### Cited Findings
- Savings (three bank field experiments, Bolivia/Peru/Philippines): reminders raised the likelihood of meeting a savings commitment by about 3 pp and total saved by about 6%; reminders that mentioned the person's specific goal were more effective than generic ones. **[R]** — [Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman 2016, Manag. Sci.](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2296)
- Medication SMS (16 RCTs, N = 2,742): OR 2.11 for adherence, roughly 50% → 67.8%; not sensitive to personalisation, two-way messaging or daily frequency; trials were short and relied heavily on self-report. **[R]** — [Thakkar et al. 2016, JAMA Intern. Med.](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.7667)
- Low-cost reminder devices (pill-bottle timers etc.) did not improve adherence in a large pragmatic trial of non-adherent patients (REMIND). **[V]** (abstract-level) — [Choudhry et al. 2017, JAMA Intern. Med.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28241271/)
- Appointment notifications meta-analysis (21 studies; 8,345 notified vs 7,731 not): attendance RR 1.23 (95% CI 1.10–1.38; 67% vs 54%, I² = 83%); no-shows RR 0.75 (0.68–0.82; 15% vs 21%). **[V]** — [Robotham et al. 2016, BMJ Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5093388/)
- Same review: more than one notification RR 1.49 (1.17–1.88) vs one notification RR 1.09 (1.00–1.18) for attendance (+25 vs +6 pp in meta-regression); no advantage of multiple reminders on the no-show outcome (p = .91). Voice slightly better than text for attendance (borderline). **[V]** — [Robotham et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5093388/)
- Earlier SMS meta-analysis: reminders increased attendance (OR ≈ 1.48 **[R]**), with no significant subgroup differences by message timing (24, 48 or 72+ hours ahead). **[V]** for the timing statement — [Guy et al. 2012, Health Serv. Res.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22091980/)
- Another meta-analysis: no significant difference in effect by when the SMS was sent, reminder frequency or content; authors say optimal timing and frequency remain unidentified. **[V]** (abstract-level) — [Boksmati et al. 2016, J. Med. Syst.](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10916-016-0452-2)
- Cochrane review: text reminders increase attendance vs no reminder and vs postal reminders, and are equivalent to phone calls. **[V]** — [Gurol-Urganci et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6485985)
- Wording matters at the margin: stating the specific cost of a missed appointment in the SMS cut no-shows from 11.1% to 8.4% in a hospital RCT. **[R]** — [Hallsworth et al. 2015, PLoS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)
- Megastudy 1 (47,306 patients, 19 text nudges before a doctor's visit): average +5% in flu vaccination (≈ 2.1 pp **[R]**); best performer reminded patients twice and said a flu shot was "reserved for you" (≈ +4.6 pp / 11% **[R]**). **[V]** for design and ranking — [Milkman et al. 2021, PNAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v118y2021pe2101165118.html)
- Megastudy 2 (689,693 Walmart pharmacy patients, 22 texts): average +2.0 pp (6.8%) over business-as-usual; top message was two texts three days apart saying a vaccine was "waiting for you"; neither experts nor lay forecasters predicted the winner. **[V]** — [Milkman et al. 2022, PNAS](https://bcfg.wharton.upenn.edu/research/a-680000-person-megastudy-of-nudges-to-encourage-vaccination-in-pharmacies/)
- Megastudy 3 (millions of CVS patients, 2024): behaviourally informed reminders raised 30-day COVID booster uptake by 21% (1.05 pp) and spilled over to +8% (0.34 pp) flu vaccination; adding free round-trip rides gave no extra benefit. **[V]** — [Milkman et al. 2024, Nature 631:179–188](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/megastudy-shows-that-reminders-boost-vaccination-but-adding-free-rides-does-not/)
- Timing within a rollout (N ≈ 93,000, UCLA Health): a text one day after eligibility raised appointments by 6.07 pp (84%) and vaccinations by 3.57 pp (26%); a second reminder a week later added a further ≈ 1.1 pp; ownership wording ("made available for you") helped; an added video did not. **[R]** — [Dai et al. 2021, Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03843-2)
- Limits: eight message variants sent to ~142,000 still-unvaccinated Rhode Islanders late in the campaign had no detectable effect, i.e. reminders do not move people who lack the intention. **[R]** — [Rabb et al. 2022, Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04526-2)
- Gym megastudy (61,293 members, 54 four-week programmes): 45% of programmes significantly raised weekly visits, by 9–27%; top performer gave micro-rewards for returning after a missed workout; only 8% produced measurable change after the four weeks ended; forecasters could not predict which would work. **[V]** — [Milkman et al. 2021, Nature 600:478–483](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/megastudies-improve-impact-applied-behavioural-science)
- Scale reality check: across 126 RCTs run by two US nudge units (≈ 23 million people) the average effect was 1.4 pp (≈ 8% relative), versus 8.7 pp (≈ 33%) in published academic nudge trials; the authors attribute most of the difference to publication bias plus low power. **[R]** — [DellaVigna & Linos 2022, Econometrica](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18709)
- Meta-analysis of choice-architecture nudges: d = 0.43 overall (200+ studies), larger for "decision structure" changes such as defaults than for information or reminder-type "decision assistance". **[R]** — [Mertens et al. 2022, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2107346118)
- Critique: re-analysis with bias correction concluded "no evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias" (pooled d ≈ 0.04 **[R]**); a companion letter argued there is no reason to expect large, consistent nudge effects given heterogeneity. **[R]** — [Maier et al. 2022, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2200300119); [Szaszi et al. 2022, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2200732119)
- App notifications, micro-randomised trial (Drink Less): a notification raised the probability of opening the app in the next hour 3.5-fold (95% CI 2.91–4.25); the effect did not decay significantly over time, but time to disengagement did not differ across notification policies. **[V]** — [Bell et al. 2023, JMIR mHealth uHealth](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)
- HeartSteps micro-randomised trial: contextually tailored activity suggestions raised step count in the following 30 minutes (≈ 14% overall, ≈ 24% for walking suggestions), with the effect shrinking over the six-week study. **[R]** — [Klasnja et al. 2019, Ann. Behav. Med.](https://doi.org/10.1093/abm/kay067)

### Inferences
- Message wording: use possession framing for the user's own item ("Your bike-service slot for tomorrow morning", "Your Bir Billing trip: the weekend is clear") rather than generic advice. This is the most consistently winning feature across two megastudies and one Nature RCT, though all three are vaccination contexts.
- Name the user's specific goal in the reminder (Karlan et al.) and, for deadline items, the concrete cost of missing it (Hallsworth et al.).
- Structure: one follow-up a few days later is evidence-based (top megastudy arms; multiple > single in Robotham); beyond two there is no evidence of added benefit, and the "Later" button supplies the user-driven version of this.
- Keep expectations and evaluation honest: at scale, plain reminders yield low single-digit pp gains, and elaborate copy variants rarely beat a simple reminder. A/B tests on wording will need large samples; for a single-user app, prefer the robust defaults above over fine-tuning.
- Reminders convert existing intentions; they do not create them. If an item has been nudged twice with no engagement, the evidence (Rabb et al.) suggests further nudges are wasted and the item should move to review/drop.

### Gaps
- All megastudy "ownership" evidence is from vaccination; no test found for personal errands or leisure goals.
- Appointment-reminder reviews could not identify an optimal lead time (see section 5).
- Whether notification effects persist beyond weeks for a personal-task app is not established; the gym megastudy's 8% persistence figure is the closest evidence.

## 4. "Reminders through association" (cue-based reminders)

### Takeaway
Tying an intention to a distinctive cue that will be physically present at the moment of opportunity raised follow-through in the original six-study paper, but the evidence is a single 2016 paper with one modest field experiment and no independent replication found.

### Cited Findings
- Six lab and field experiments; the approach "can substantially raise follow-through on intentions"; paper carries open-data and open-materials badges. **[V]** — [Rogers & Milkman 2016, Psychol. Sci. 27(7):973–986](https://repository.upenn.edu/oid_papers/294)
- Field experiment: ~500 coffee-shop customers got a coupon valid two days later; those told a stuffed alien would be sitting at the register as their reminder redeemed at about 24% vs 17% without the cue (≈ 7 pp; reported as roughly a 36–40% relative increase). **[S]** — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/hacking-memory-to-follow-through-with-intentions.html)
- The cue has to be distinctive relative to its surroundings and encountered where and when the action is possible; people undervalue such cues when offered them. **[S]** — [Knowledge at Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/stop-tracks-hack-forgetfulness/)

### Inferences
- For errands the app cannot time precisely, pair the note with a cue the user will meet at the point of action ("When you see the empty bottle on the shelf…", "When you park the bike tonight…"). This is consistent with implementation-intention and prospective-memory mechanisms, which are better replicated than this specific paper.
- Treat as promising, not established: one paper, one field site, n ≈ 250 per arm.

### Gaps
- No direct replication, meta-analysis or standardised effect size was found in this search.
- No evidence found on digital analogues (e.g. location-triggered notifications as the "distinctive cue").

## 5. Prospective memory: event- vs time-based cues and reminder timing relative to the opportunity

### Takeaway
Remembering to act is easier when an external event cues it than when the person must self-initiate at a time, and even delays of seconds to minutes between the cue and the chance to act cause forgetting; this is the strongest theoretical basis for delivering reminders when action is immediately possible. Clinical reminder trials, however, have not found a reliable optimal lead time between 24 and 72 hours.

### Cited Findings
- Event-based prospective memory tasks (act when X appears) rely on external cues, whereas time-based tasks require self-initiated monitoring and are more failure-prone; the distinction originates with Einstein & McDaniel. **[R]** — [Einstein & McDaniel 1990, JEP:LMC](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.16.4.717); summarised **[V]** in [2025 study on strategic reminder setting for time-based intentions](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12589243/)
- Multiprocess account: retrieval is spontaneous and cheap when the cue is salient and "focal" to what the person is already doing; otherwise it needs costly monitoring. **[R]** — [McDaniel & Einstein 2000, Appl. Cogn. Psychol.](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.775)
- Delayed-execute paradigm: when people retrieve an intention but must wait even 5–40 seconds under a demanding task before acting, forgetting is rapid. **[R]** — [Einstein et al. 2003, JEP: Applied](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.9.3.147)
- Intention offloading review: setting external reminders is "highly effective", is guided by metacognitive beliefs, and people show stable systematic biases in when they choose to offload. **[V]** (as characterised in search results) — [Gilbert et al. 2023, Psychon. Bull. Rev.](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-022-02139-4)
- Offloading for time-based intentions behaves like other offloading: it improves performance, rises with cognitive load and depends on metacognition; real-world offloading may be especially common for time-based tasks, yet experiments have mostly studied event-based ones. **[V]** — [2025 study, PMC12589243](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12589243/)
- Appointment reminders: no significant subgroup differences by lead time of 24, 48 or 72+ hours **[V]** — [Guy et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22091980/); no pooled timing comparison possible in the BMJ Open review (9 studies ≤ 48 h, 3 > 48 h); five of eight multi-reminder studies sent one before and one after the 48-hour mark **[V]** — [Robotham et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5093388/)
- An earlier systematic review of phone/SMS reminders found no difference in non-attendance between reminders sent the day before and the week before. **[S]** (search-result characterisation; article not opened) — [review, PMC3188816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3188816/)
- When the action can be taken immediately on receipt, earlier is better: day-1 reminder ≫ day-8 reminder in the UCLA vaccination RCT. **[R]** — [Dai et al. 2021, Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03843-2)

### Inferences
- Two different timing problems should be handled differently. (1) Scheduled commitments (service the bike tomorrow 9 am): the evidence does not favour any particular lead time between a day and three days; a sensible pattern from the multi-reminder trials is one message early enough to prepare (evening before) plus one at the point of action. (2) Unscheduled errands (beard oil): there is no deadline, so the only good moment is when acting is possible within seconds of reading, e.g. when the user is already shopping or on the phone with time free. A reminder that arrives mid-meeting recreates the delayed-execute failure.
- Convert time-based intentions into event-based ones where possible ("when you leave work" rather than "at 6 pm"); this is what the prospective-memory literature predicts will be more reliably acted on.
- "Quick-action reminders" should include the action itself (a one-tap order link, a call button) so the cue-to-action delay is near zero.
- The "opportunity nudge" tied to an outside event (clear weekend plus good weather) is an event-based cue by construction, which is the better-supported cue type.

### Gaps
- No field experiment found that directly compares "evening before" with "morning of" for the same task.
- No direct test found of opportunity-triggered (context-aware) reminders versus fixed-time reminders for personal tasks; the inference above rests on lab prospective-memory findings plus health field data.
- Lab prospective-memory effects are robust paradigms but mostly small-sample; no bias-corrected meta-analytic estimate was retrieved.

## 6. Timing effects: fresh starts, temporal landmarks, day and time of day, decision fatigue

### Takeaway
People spontaneously start aspirational activities after temporal landmarks (archival evidence, well documented), but deliberately exploiting this in messages has a mixed record: one field experiment worked for scheduling a future commitment, one field RCT by the same group was null, and anticipated landmarks can reduce current effort. "Decision fatigue"/ego depletion has failed large pre-registered replications and should not drive design.

### Cited Findings
- Three archival field studies: Google searches for "diet", university gym visits, and stickK commitment contracts all rise after temporal landmarks; gym visits were higher at the start of the week (≈ +33%), month (≈ +14%), year (≈ +12%), semester (≈ +47%) and after birthdays (≈ +7.5%) **[R]** for the percentages. Correlational; the authors say the data give "imperfect insights into the mechanisms". **[V]** for design and caveat — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014, Manag. Sci.](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901)
- Follow-up online lab experiments: framing a date as a meaningful new beginning increased intended goal initiation. Small-scale, intention outcomes. **[R]** — [Dai, Milkman & Riis 2015, Psychol. Sci.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615605818)
- Field experiment (N = 6,082 university employees): describing a future savings-increase date as a fresh start ("after your next birthday", "first day of spring") raised take-up of the delayed increase without reducing immediate increases, and raised contributions over eight months; described as the first experimental field demonstration. **[V]** — [Beshears, Dai, Milkman & Benartzi 2021, OBHDP](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:167:y:2021:i:c:p:72-87)
- In that study the New Year's framing did not produce the expected response. **[S]** — [Center for Retirement Research summary](https://crr.bc.edu/squared-away/viewing-retirement-saving-as-a-fresh-start/)
- Null field result: a trial of medication reminders tied to fresh-start dates did not improve adherence. **[S]** (headline only; researcher recalls this as Dai et al. 2017, JAMA Cardiology) — [Cardiovascular Business report](https://cardiovascularbusiness.com/node/81291)
- Backfire: making an upcoming fresh start salient caused people already pursuing a goal to slack off beforehand. **[S]** — [UCLA Anderson Review brief](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/fresh-start-framing-boosts-retirement-plan-participation/)
- Ego depletion multi-lab replications: 23 labs, N = 2,141, d = 0.04 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.15) **[R]** — [Hagger et al. 2016, Perspect. Psychol. Sci.](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873); 36 labs, N ≈ 3,500, d ≈ 0.06 **[R]** — [Vohs et al. 2021, Psychol. Sci.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733)
- The widely cited "hungry judges" parole study **[R]** — [Danziger et al. 2011, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1018033108) — has been challenged on the grounds that case ordering was not random (e.g. unrepresented prisoners heard last before breaks). **[R]** — [Weinshall-Margel & Shapard 2011, PNAS letter](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1110910108)

### Inferences
- Supported use: when the user picks "Later", offer landmark-anchored options ("Monday", "the 1st", "after your trip") as the default snooze choices. This matches the one positive field experiment (choosing a future start date framed as a fresh start) and the archival pattern.
- Not supported: adding "It's a fresh start!" copy to a reminder for something that can be done now. The only field RCT of that design found was null, and a salient future landmark may license delay.
- The weekly review is plausibly best placed at a natural week boundary (Sunday evening or Monday morning) given the start-of-week uptick, but that uptick is observational and concerns aspirational behaviours; no experiment found compares review days.
- Do not build "user is depleted in the evening, so do not ask" logic on ego-depletion theory. Any time-of-day rule should come from the user's own response data.

### Gaps
- No pre-registered direct replication of the 2014 archival fresh-start findings was located (search returned only the originals and popular coverage).
- No rigorous evidence was retrieved on day-of-week or time-of-day effects on acting on personal reminders; the researcher recalls one micro-randomised trial reporting notifications working best around midday on weekends (Bidargaddi et al. 2018, JMIR mHealth) but did not verify it this session.
- Hennecke & Converse (2017) and Koo et al. (2020) on boundary effects were not opened; only secondary characterisation is given.

## 7. Friction, task size, goal gradient and "tiny habits"

### Takeaway
Removing steps is among the most powerful and best-replicated levers in applied behavioural science (defaults, pre-filled forms, a specific channel to act); small-first-step and goal-gradient ideas have supportive but thinner evidence; "Tiny Habits" as a branded method has no independent trial evidence that this search could find.

### Cited Findings
- Channel factors classic: students given a campus map and asked to plan a time got tetanus shots at 28% vs 3% with a fear appeal alone (small 1965 sample). **[R]** — [Leventhal, Singer & Jones 1965, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0022089)
- Defaults: automatic enrolment raised 401(k) participation among new hires from about 37% to 86%. **[R]** — [Madrian & Shea 2001, QJE](https://doi.org/10.1162/003355301753265543)
- Defaults meta-analysis: 58 studies, N ≈ 73,700, d = 0.68, with large heterogeneity across domains. **[R]** — [Jachimowicz et al. 2019, Behav. Public Policy](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2018.43)
- Doing the step for people beats telling them: tax preparers pre-filling the financial-aid form raised college enrolment by about 8 pp; information alone had no effect. **[R]** — [Bettinger et al. 2012, QJE](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs017)
- Structural changes outperform informational nudges in the (bias-uncorrected) nudge meta-analysis. **[R]** — [Mertens et al. 2022, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2107346118)
- In the 2024 megastudy, removing a large practical barrier (free rides to the pharmacy) added nothing beyond a reminder, so friction removal only helps if it targets the binding constraint. **[V]** — [Milkman et al. 2024, Nature](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/megastudy-shows-that-reminders-boost-vaccination-but-adding-free-rides-does-not/)
- Sub-goal framing field experiment (≈ 9,100 crisis-line volunteers): reframing a 200-hour commitment as 4 hours a week (or 8 per fortnight) raised hours volunteered by about 7–8% over 12 weeks. **[R]** — [Rai et al. 2023, J. Appl. Psychol.](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001040)
- Goal gradient: coffee-card customers bought more frequently as they neared the free coffee; a card with illusory head-start progress was completed faster. **[R]** — [Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006, J. Mark. Res.](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39)
- Endowed progress: car-wash cards needing 10 stamps with 2 pre-filled were completed by 34% of customers vs 19% for an 8-stamp blank card (single field study, ~300 cards). **[R]** — [Nunes & Drèze 2006, J. Consum. Res.](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480)

### Inferences
- "Let's do it" should land on the action already set up (deep link to the product, a drafted message to the mechanic, a pre-filled calendar slot), not on a text describing what to do. Pre-filling is where the large, replicated effects are.
- Suggested next steps should be sized so the first one is completable in one sitting, and multi-step projects (motorcycle trip) should show progress with early steps already ticked where true (e.g. "dates checked"). Evidence for the progress display is two mid-2000s marketing field studies, so treat as low-cost and plausible, not proven.
- Before reducing a friction, ask whether it is the real blocker; the free-rides null shows that removing a non-binding barrier does nothing.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed controlled trial of the Tiny Habits method was found (not specifically searched beyond the Fogg queries); its claims should be treated as practitioner guidance.
- No direct evidence retrieved on "one-click" completion for personal reminders.
- Goal-gradient and endowed-progress replication status was not checked.

## 8. Fogg Behavior Model versus better-validated frameworks

### Takeaway
B = MAP is a useful design checklist that is widely cited but thinly tested: it originated in a 2009 conference paper, published studies mostly use it to design interventions and do not test its predictions, and its components overlap with COM-B. Habit research with behavioural data shows habit formation is slow, variable and context-cue dependent, with no fixed number of days.

### Cited Findings
- The model was introduced in a conference paper (Persuasive 2009), not a peer-reviewed behavioural-science journal. **[R]** — [Fogg 2009, Persuasive '09](https://doi.org/10.1145/1541948.1541999)
- Most published studies use the model as a design framework and do not directly test its predictions; it has "rarely been tested formally in health settings". **[S]** — [The Behavioral Scientist overview](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/?p=3058)
- A 2025 scoping review of the model in health interventions used a narrative synthesis and concluded it is an "effective framework"; the search found no test of the model's core claim (that motivation, ability and a prompt must coincide) within it. **[V]** (record-level; full text behind a redirect) — [BMC Public Health 2025, doi 10.1186/s12889-025-24525-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-025-24525-y)
- Criticisms recorded in secondary sources: over-simplification, neglect of unconscious, social and environmental influences, and its role in attention-capturing product design. **[S]** — [The Decision Lab reference guide](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/psychology/fogg-behavior-model)
- COM-B / Behaviour Change Wheel: derived from a systematic review of 19 existing behaviour-change frameworks; behaviour arises from capability, opportunity and motivation; linked to nine intervention functions. **[R]** — [Michie, van Stralen & West 2011, Implement. Sci.](https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-5908-6-42)
- Habit formation: in 96 volunteers repeating a chosen daily behaviour, automaticity plateaued after a median of 66 days (range 18–254); missing a single day did not materially harm formation. Small self-selected sample, self-reported automaticity. **[R]** — [Lally et al. 2010, Eur. J. Soc. Psychol.](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- Systematic review (20 studies, 2,601 adults): only four studies measured time to habit; medians 59–66 days, means 106–154 days, individual range 4–335 days. **[V]** — [Singh et al. 2024, Healthcare (record)](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fe6ba93e-1d6b-42b8-9ee7-a231a64ff006)
- Machine-learning analysis of tens of millions of gym check-ins and hospital hand-sanitiser uses: gym habits took months to form, handwashing weeks; no "magic number"; behaviour became predictable from context variables. **[R]** — [Buyalskaya et al. 2023, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2216115120)
- Habits are triggered by stable context cues largely independent of current goals **[R]** — [Wood & Neal 2007, Psychol. Rev.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.843); roughly 43% of daily actions in diary studies were performed almost daily in the same context **[R]** — [Wood, Quinn & Kashy 2002, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.6.1281)

### Inferences
- Use B = MAP as a pre-send checklist only (is the user plausibly willing, able right now, and is this the prompt?), which is consistent with better-evidenced findings elsewhere in these notes; do not cite it as the scientific basis for the product.
- COM-B is the more defensible vocabulary for diagnosing why a note is stuck: capability (does not know the first step), opportunity (no time, shop closed, weather), motivation (no longer wants it). Each maps onto a different button or message type.
- Most captured thoughts are one-off intentions, not habits; habit-formation timelines matter only for recurring items, where the app should expect months and rely on a stable context cue, not on streak counts.

### Gaps
- The BMC Public Health review's full text was not accessible, so the number and design of included studies is unreported here.
- COM-B is itself a framework and this search did not retrieve evidence on its predictive validity; "better validated" here means more systematically derived and more widely used in peer-reviewed intervention development, not experimentally confirmed.

## 9. Weekly review, plan-making and progress monitoring

### Takeaway
Monitoring goal progress has solid meta-analytic support (d ≈ 0.40, larger when progress is recorded or reported). The claim that making a plan frees the mind from an unfinished goal rests on one multi-study 2011 lab paper with no replication found. The "weekly review" ritual itself has essentially no direct experimental evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 138 experiments (N ≈ 19,950): interventions that increased progress monitoring improved goal attainment (d+ = 0.40); effects were larger when outcomes were physically recorded or reported/made public. **[R]** — [Harkin et al. 2016, Psychol. Bull.](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)
- "Consider it done!": unfulfilled goals caused intrusive thoughts, heightened accessibility of goal words and worse performance on an unrelated task; writing a specific plan eliminated these effects; the benefit depended on the plan being earnest. Series of small lab studies. **[V]** — [Masicampo & Baumeister 2011, JPSP 101(4):667–683](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/)
- Replication status: two targeted searches found no independent or registered replication of the 2011 plan-making studies. **[V]** (absence of evidence in search) — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/)
- Getting Things Done: the main academic treatment is a theoretical paper arguing the method is consistent with distributed-cognition principles; it reports no experiment. **[R]** — [Heylighen & Vidal 2008, Long Range Planning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2008.09.004)
- Related lab evidence on offloading plans: writing a to-do list for five minutes at bedtime shortened sleep onset compared with writing about completed tasks (N = 57, polysomnography). **[R]** — [Scullin et al. 2018, JEP: General](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000374)
- Small RCT (84 undergraduates, five weeks, conversational agent): progress monitoring raised goal attainment; reflection alone did not. **[V]** (abstract-level) — [TU Delft repository, 2025](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:4cf22026-32ed-4a51-bd36-5aebf3c6e232)
- A 2023 workplace field experiment (208 participants, 947 weekly diaries) reportedly found weekly planning reduced unfinished tasks and after-work rumination. **[S]** (vendor blog; primary not verified) — [weekplan.net](https://weekplan.net/weekly-review-productivity/)

### Inferences
- The weekly review is best justified as a progress-monitoring device, not by GTD: show each open item's status and ask the user to record an explicit outcome (done / next step chosen / dropped). Recording is the moderator with support.
- A review that only invites reflection ("how do you feel about these?") is the weaker design; a review that ends with a concrete next step or a drop decision per item is the stronger one.
- Capturing a thought and immediately attaching a small plan may reduce the mental nagging of open loops, which is a plausible user benefit, but it should be presented as plausible, not proven (single unreplicated paper).

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence found on review cadence (weekly vs other) or on the GTD weekly review specifically.
- Harkin et al. figures are recalled; the workplace weekly-planning study is known only second-hand.

## 10. Reactance, autonomy and reminder wording

### Takeaway
Controlling, forceful language reliably produces reactance (anger plus counter-arguing) and lower intention to comply, with a small-to-moderate effect in meta-analyses; nearly all of that evidence is on attitudes and intentions in lab-style message studies, not on behaviour after app reminders. No trial was found on letting users choose their own reminder times.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis (33 studies, 146 effect sizes, 2005–2024): high freedom-threatening language increased anger, negative cognitions and reactance vs low-threat language (r ≈ .20); gain vs loss framing made no significant difference to reactance. **[V]** (abstract-level) — [Message effects on psychological reactance: meta-analyses](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/message-effects-on-psychological-reactance-meta-analyses/)
- 2026 meta-analysis in Journal of Communication (35 studies, N = 10,658): forceful, freedom-threatening health language raised state reactance, which was associated with less agreement and lower intention to change; moderated by sample type, age and gender; effects modest. **[S]** — [PsyPost report](https://www.psypost.org/forceful-language-makes-people-resist-health-advice/)
- Reactance is best modelled as intertwined anger and negative cognitions, per an earlier meta-analytic review. **[R]** — [Rains 2013, Hum. Commun. Res.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2012.01443.x)
- Experiment: controlling language in a healthy-eating message induced more reactance than autonomy-supportive language, lowering intentions. **[S]** (search-result characterisation) — [Springer article, s12144-019-00588-0](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-019-00588-0)
- Self-determination-theory interventions in health show small-to-medium positive effects on behaviour at end of intervention and smaller effects at follow-up (g roughly 0.45 and 0.28; recalled, verify). **[R]** — [Ntoumanis et al. 2021, Health Psychol. Rev.](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2020.1718529)
- Planning prompts are noted to preserve autonomy because they do not tell people what to do. **[V]** — [Rogers et al. 2015](https://behavioralpolicy.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/BSP_vol1is2_-Rogers.pdf)
- User-set reminder times: a sleep self-help app study compared reminders at user-chosen times with reminders at system-inferred interruptible moments; the authors had no directional hypothesis, and the rationale for self-set times is that users know best when they can act. No clear advantage was established. **[V]** (abstract-level) — [Horsch et al., PMC5686282](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5686282/)

### Inferences
- Wording: suggestions and questions over directives ("Want to sort the bike service this morning?" not "You must service your bike"), no "you should / need to / don't forget", and no implication that the user has failed. The message is about the user's own stated wish, which can be said explicitly ("You mentioned…").
- The four buttons are themselves an autonomy-supportive structure (real choice including refusal); the evidence supports keeping "Not interested" equal in visual weight to the others.
- Ownership framing (section 3) and autonomy support are compatible: "your slot is waiting" asserts possession, not obligation.
- Offer the user control over reminder times as a preference and trust feature; do not claim it improves follow-through, since no supporting trial was found.

### Gaps
- No evidence found specifically on guilt-inducing reminder wording and subsequent behaviour.
- No randomised comparison of user-chosen vs system-chosen reminder times with a behavioural outcome was found.
- Reactance evidence is overwhelmingly about one-off persuasive health messages, not repeated personal reminders; cumulative effects over many notifications are asserted in commentary but not measured in the sources retrieved.

## 11. Abandoning intentions: goal disengagement

### Takeaway
The capacity to let go of unattainable goals and re-engage with new ones is associated with better wellbeing and health in a sizeable, mostly correlational literature; this supports making "drop it" a legitimate, guilt-free outcome, with the caveat that the research concerns unattainable life goals, not minor whims.

### Cited Findings
- Across three studies, people better able to disengage from unattainable goals and to re-engage with alternatives reported higher subjective wellbeing. **[R]** — [Wrosch, Scheier, Miller, Schulz & Carver 2003, Pers. Soc. Psychol. Bull.](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167203256921)
- Meta-analysis (30+ samples): goal disengagement and re-engagement capacities show protective, small-to-medium associations with psychological and physical health / quality of life. **[V]** (abstract and secondary characterisation; pooled values not retrieved) — [Barlow, Wrosch & McGrath 2020, J. Pers. (summary)](https://ehps.net/ehp/index.php/contents/article/view/2727)
- Larger 2025 synthesis: 1,421 effect sizes from 235 studies on goal adjustment (disengagement, re-engagement and related processes), proposing a conceptual model of when people adjust goals. **[V]** (record-level) — [Riddell et al. 2025, Nature Human Behaviour](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/506833/2/s41562-025-02312-4.pdf)
- Sheeran & Webb's framework lists failing to disengage from futile or completed goals as one of three classes of self-regulation failure, alongside failing to start and failing to continue. **[R]** — [Sheeran & Webb 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12265)
- Unresolved goals keep intruding on unrelated tasks until planned or resolved (single 2011 paper, see section 9). **[V]** — [Masicampo & Baumeister 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/)

### Inferences
- "Not interested" should close the loop cleanly and neutrally ("Dropped. You can always jot it again."), with no streaks, guilt copy or "are you sure?" friction. Dropping is a valid resolution of an open loop.
- The weekly review should actively offer dropping for stale items, and where apt suggest a substitute ("Skip the wardrobe light; keep the trip?"), since the literature pairs disengagement with re-engagement.
- Do not over-claim: the evidence is about trait capacities and unattainable major goals, largely correlational. It shows letting go is not harmful and is linked to wellbeing; it does not show that an app prompting people to drop small intentions improves wellbeing.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes from Barlow et al. 2020 and Riddell et al. 2025 were not retrieved.
- No experimental study found on prompting people to abandon minor personal intentions or on the wording of a "drop it" option.
