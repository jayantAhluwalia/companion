# Actionable nudges beat finely timed ones

The evidence says a reminder of something a person already intends to do works by a small margin, and that what the message is about and how easy it makes the action decide whether it gets acted on more than the clock time it arrives. Large field trials put the lift from a plain reminder at roughly 1 to 5 percentage points for one-off actions; app trials show a prompt strongly moves the immediate response (3.5 times more likely to open an app within the hour) and barely moves anything harder or later. Timing research finds that well-chosen moments mostly make people respond faster; the only consistent clock-time result is that early morning is the worst slot, and the strongest timing signals come from phone sensors this app does not have. The proposed strategy's structure (a separate deadline lane, event-triggered opportunity nudges, one-tap buttons, a review for everything else, piggybacking, back-off) is consistent with that evidence, but none of its numbers (1 a day, about 3 a week, one weekly review) has direct support, its worst case sends more than the current rule allows, and its "before work" window sits in the slot the evidence rates lowest. No study tests reminders of a person's own stated wishes sent by a sensor-less chat companion, so every effect size below is borrowed from health apps, vaccination campaigns, marketing pushes and phone-wide notification studies. The priority changes are to hold total volume under one shared weekly budget, send only when the person can act now with the action already prepared, write the back-off as explicit rules, and learn timing from a simple per-window tally with deliberate randomisation, because one user at a few nudges a week produces too little data for anything cleverer. Two acted-on nudges out of two is a good start that does not yet distinguish a good strategy from a lucky one.

## A plain reminder adds 1 to 5 points, and only to live intentions

A note on provenance first. The researchers tagged each figure. Below, "(recalled)" marks a number given from a researcher's memory of the paper with the source not re-opened, and "(secondary)" marks a number taken from a press release, blog or another paper's summary. Neither kind is verified against the original; check them before quoting. Unmarked figures were read in the paper's abstract or full text.

The starting point is that intentions convert about half the time. One review concludes that intentions turn into action about half the time (recalled) ([Sheeran & Webb 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12265)). That estimate comes mostly from health behaviours and student samples; nothing measures the gap for errands, trips and ideas. It still frames the job correctly: the app's users have already decided, so the work is prompting and easing, not persuading.

What a reminder buys is well established and modest. In a megastudy of **689,693 pharmacy patients, 22 text reminders raised vaccination by an average of 2.0 percentage points**, and the best performer was two texts three days apart saying a vaccine was "waiting for you" ([Milkman et al. 2022](https://bcfg.wharton.upenn.edu/research/a-680000-person-megastudy-of-nudges-to-encourage-vaccination-in-pharmacies/)). A later megastudy on millions of patients found reminders raised booster uptake by 1.05 points, and adding free rides to the pharmacy added nothing ([Milkman et al. 2024](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/megastudy-shows-that-reminders-boost-vaccination-but-adding-free-rides-does-not/)). A meta-analysis of appointment notifications found attendance of 67% with a notification against 54% without (risk ratio 1.23, with high heterogeneity), and more than one notification beat a single one on attendance (risk ratio 1.49 against 1.09) though not on no-shows ([Robotham et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5093388/)). The sobering comparison is that across 126 trials run by two US nudge units the average effect was 1.4 points, against 8.7 points in published academic nudge trials (recalled) ([DellaVigna & Linos 2022](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18709)). Small studies overstate; plan on low single digits per nudge type at best.

Reminders convert intentions that exist and do nothing for those that do not. Eight message variants sent to about 142,000 still-unvaccinated people late in a campaign had no detectable effect (recalled) ([Rabb et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04526-2)). An item the user has gone cold on will not be revived by more nudges.

App trials that randomise each individual prompt show the same shape in finer grain: the effect is large on whatever is closest to the notification and fades with distance. In the Drink Less trial, 12 to 13% of users opened the app within an hour of an 8 pm notification against 3.6% without one (**relative risk 3.52**), but over 24 hours the relative risk fell to 1.26, and median time to abandoning the app did not differ significantly between notification policies ([Bell et al. 2023](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2023/1/e38342)). In a workplace wellbeing app with 1,255 users, a push raised next-24-hour use by 3.9% in relative terms ([Bidargaddi et al. 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6293241/)). In HeartSteps, a walking suggestion raised the next half hour's step count by 24% averaged over six weeks, but by 107% at the start of the study, and a second message type had no detectable effect at all ([Klasnja et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)). Prompts for harder outcomes often show nothing: activity suggestions "had no effect" in a 16-week trial with bariatric patients ([Klasnja et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1093/tbm/ibaa040)), and messages in a 2026 cannabis-use trial raised engagement without changing use ([Walton et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lana.2026.101524)). Meta-analyses of whole just-in-time intervention packages report much larger pooled effects, but those measure entire programmes against controls in small, heterogeneous studies; a review of physical-activity versions found that "no study was sufficiently powered to detect any effects" ([Hardeman et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-019-0792-7)), and a 2026 sleep review found no difference between adaptive and non-adaptive momentary interventions ([Xi et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2026.109349)).

Three message features have support beyond a bare reminder, each with a caveat. Ownership framing ("reserved for you", "waiting for you") won two megastudies, both about vaccination ([Milkman et al. 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v118y2021pe2101165118.html)). Planning prompts that ask when and where add roughly 1 to 4 points in field trials (recalled) ([Milkman et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1103170108)), and a 2024 meta-analysis of 642 tests found if-then plans effective with d between 0.27 and 0.66 ([Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)); but a well-powered field test on gym attendance produced a "tightly estimated null" ([Carrera et al. 2018](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24959)), so planning suits one-off actions more than repeated ones. One trial found a plan supplied by the experimenter worked as well as a self-written one ([Armitage 2009](https://peeref.com/works/10020182)), which is the only direct support for the app drafting "Next steps", and it is a single alcohol study. Removing steps has the largest effects in applied behavioural science; for example, pre-filling a financial-aid form raised college enrolment by about 8 points where information alone did nothing (recalled) ([Bettinger et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs017)). The free-rides null shows the limit: removing a barrier helps only when it is the barrier that binds.

Several popular ideas rest on thin ground. "Fresh start" framing has archival support and one positive field experiment on scheduling a future commitment ([Beshears et al. 2021](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:167:y:2021:i:c:p:72-87)), but a field trial of fresh-start medication reminders was null (secondary) ([Cardiovascular Business](https://cardiovascularbusiness.com/node/81291)). Ego depletion, the basis for "don't ask in the evening" rules, failed a 23-lab replication with d = 0.04 (recalled) ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873)). The Fogg Behavior Model is a design checklist; the researchers found no test of its core claim, including in a 2025 scoping review of its use in health interventions ([BMC Public Health 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-025-24525-y)). Cue-based "reminders through association" rest on one 2016 paper with no replication found ([Rogers & Milkman 2016](https://repository.upenn.edu/oid_papers/294)).

## Good moments speed the reply; content decides the action

The best-replicated timing result concerns speed. Delivering a notification at a natural break (just after a call, a text or a sub-task) cut response times by roughly 25 to 50% in five studies from three research groups. In the cleanest mobile study, 20 people accepted a notification in a median 10 to 19 seconds just after a call or text against 36 seconds at a random time ([Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)). In a 687,840-user deployment, break-timed pushes cut response time by 49.7% (secondary), while the gain in click-through was not statistically significant on an independent reading ([Tsubouchi & Okoshi 2017](https://interruptions.net/literature/Tsubouchi-UbiComp17.pdf); [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)). The effect on whether people respond at all is small: in Fischer's study the association between timing and responding was weak (phi = .076; about 73% against 66% by the researcher's own arithmetic from the paper's counts), and participants did not rate the well-timed notifications as better timed.

Sensor-based timing models shift the odds modestly. The only randomised live test found that a model using time of day, lock state, activity and similar signals raised response within 10 minutes from 28.4% to about 39%, and any response from 65.6% to about 74%; a per-user adaptive version did no better than the fixed population model, though a bug delayed its start ([Mishra et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.08302)). A smaller trial found sensor-driven timing no better than a random time inside a fixed 5 pm to 8 pm window, and was underpowered ([Morrison et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0169162)). The strongest in-the-moment signals in all these studies (screen unlocked, phone just used, walking) need sensors. The most transferable finding needs none: in a 337-user study, **adding four features describing each user's own past responses lifted precision from 7.1% to 21.8%, more than 197 sensor features achieved** ([Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)). That is one study with an unusual, incentivised outcome, so the size of the gain will not carry over, but the direction favours learning from this user's history.

Clock time itself explains little. Pielot's study found conversion "largely the same" through the day and "only lower during the early hours of the day (7-8 am)". A Swiss chatbot study of 189 people found fast replies most likely between 10 am and 6 pm, less likely after 6 pm, and lowest on weekend mornings before 10 am ([Künzler et al. 2019](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/dfk/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf)). A randomised trial's exploratory analysis put the largest effect at 12:30 pm on weekends, with a weekday-weekend difference that was not significant (p = .18) ([Bidargaddi et al. 2018](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2018/11/e10123/)). A study of 126,000 shopping-app users found time of day had "virtually no impact" on how fast people engaged (secondary, as summarised by [Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)). Evening results conflict: worst for quick replies in the Swiss study, yet the slot chosen in Drink Less and Morrison because people use their phones then. The defensible rule is exclusionary: **avoid sleep hours and early morning; between mid-morning and evening no window is established as better**. Day-of-week results point in both directions.

Two findings bear directly on using a stated routine. In an 11-person experiment, messages sent at times each participant had nominated as good produced no better reaction than other times, while interesting content had a large effect (partial eta-squared = .76); the authors concluded people are bad at predicting when they will be receptive ([Fischer et al. 2010](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI10.pdf)). A 2026 qualitative study of routine-derived schedules reports the same mismatch between schedule availability and "fluctuating psychological receptivity" ([Jayasiriwardene et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09294)). The first study is old and tiny and the second has no numbers, so this is weak evidence, but it points one way: a stated routine is better at ruling windows out than at picking the moment.

Content, sender and effort govern acceptance. In a study of 20 users' everyday notifications, people who dismissed one cited content that was not urgent, important or useful in roughly 38 to 43% of cases each, and being busy in 19.6%; the top reason for clicking was "At this moment, I was free" (37.2%), followed by sender importance and useful content ([Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)). One-tap multiple-choice responses were completed in a median 29 seconds against 53 for free text ([Fischer et al. 2011](https://interruptions.net/literature/Fischer-MobileHCI11.pdf)). Person-to-person messages are opened about 64% of the time against 16% for non-social apps ([Pielot, Vradi & Park 2018](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Pielot-MobileHCI18.pdf)). A nudge about the user's own stated wish, with buttons, sits on the favourable side of every one of these factors, though none of these studies tested self-authored reminders, and the same Mehrotra study found non-person senders rated the most disruptive. Whether a chat companion reads as a contact or as a system is unknown.

Prospective-memory research supplies the theory for acting-moment delivery: remembering is easier when an external event cues the action than when the person must self-initiate at a time, and in lab tasks even a 5 to 40 second delay between recalling an intention and being able to act causes rapid forgetting (recalled) ([Einstein et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.9.3.147)). No field experiment compares event-triggered reminders with fixed-time ones for personal tasks.

## No study supports one a day, three a week or a weekly digest

The researchers looked for dose-response evidence and found none that fits. No trial randomises the number of prompts and reports a curve for behaviour or retention, no study covers reminder frequency in to-do or personal-goal apps, and there is **no evidence for or against "about 3 a week" for any reminder type**. Caps in the research literature (one to five a day) were set by investigator judgement about burden.

What exists is a pattern across unlike studies. The effect per prompt falls as recent dose rises: HeartSteps, at up to five prompts a day, saw its effect shrink from 107% to a 24% average, and a re-analysis found higher recent dosage went with smaller immediate effects ([Liao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.03539)). At one prompt a day, Drink Less detected no significant decay over 30 days, but was underpowered for it; its confidence interval runs from no loss to losing about half the effect. In HIV medication trials, weekly texts raised adherence (53% against 40% of controls) while daily texts did not (41% against 40%) (secondary) ([aidsmap report of Pop-Eleches et al. 2011](https://www.aidsmap.com/news/jan-2011/weekly-text-messages-improve-hiv-drug-adherence-kenya)), and a meta-analysis found larger effects when messages came less often than daily ([Finitsis et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3914915)). Against that, a small exploratory trial found daily notifications were viewed and acted on more than one every three days without deterring use ([Morrison et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0169162)).

Habituation to repeated, similar alerts is real and has known mitigations. After 15 days of repeated security warnings, users adhered to identical warnings 55% of the time and to warnings that changed appearance 76% of the time, by the university's summary of the paper ([Vance et al. 2018](https://marriott.byu.edu/stories/faculty-research/paying-attention-to-notifications-1412)). Among clinicians, the likelihood of accepting a reminder fell by 30% for each additional reminder in the same patient encounter, an observational result ([Ancker et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5387195)). Variation, rest periods and fewer repeats about the same item are the supported counter-measures; stacking several nudges into one moment is contraindicated.

Industry experiments point toward fewer, better-selected messages, without publishing thresholds. LinkedIn's one-week test of randomly dropping half its emails cost 2.6% of page views, while the send-everything group produced 45% more unsubscribes and spam reports ([LinkedIn Engineering](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/archive/less-is-more-optimizing-email-volume-part-1)). Meta reports that sending only top-rated notifications lost visits at first and gained them after about a year, with no magnitudes published (secondary, read through a repost) ([Analytics at Meta, reposted](https://exde601e.blogspot.com/2023/01/notifications-why-less-is-more.html)). The only published frequency thresholds are vendor surveys in which somewhere between 22% and 46% of respondents, depending on wave and question, say two to five pushes a week from one app would make them disable notifications or stop using it; the summaries disagree with each other and concern generic apps ([MarketingProfs summary of Localytics](https://www.marketingprofs.com/charts/2018/33486/how-many-mobile-push-notifications-are-too-many)). These surveys do not show three a week is harmful for personally relevant content. They do show it is not self-evidently safe for low-value content.

Batching and digests have indirect support only. One two-week trial of 237 people found that delivering all phone notifications in three daily batches improved attention (d = −0.65) and stress (d = −0.56), hourly batching did nothing, and removing notifications entirely raised anxiety (d = 0.56) ([Fitz et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016)). No replication of the batching arms was found, and batching a whole phone three times a day is a different intervention from one app's weekly review. **No controlled evaluation of a weekly digest or weekly review exists** for productivity, habit or companion apps, nor of keep/snooze/drop buttons. The nearest support for the review is that progress monitoring improves goal attainment (d = 0.40, larger when progress is recorded; recalled) ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)). Two cautions apply: in a year-long study of 295 snooze-tool users, people deferred mostly to later the same day or the next morning and rarely beyond two days ([Weber et al. 2018](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Weber-MobileHCI18.pdf)), and the replicated cost of withholding notifications is worry about missing things.

## The proposal's structure holds up; its numbers and morning slot do not

| Part of the proposed strategy | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Deadline lane, always sent, timed to the deadline | Type supported; "always" and repeat count untested | Reminders raise attendance; two touches beat one; no best lead time between 1 and 3 days; repeats about the same item lose acceptance |
| Opportunity lane triggered by an outside event | Type supported; "1 a day" unsupported | Event cues are the better-supported cue type; event-related notifications are the ones users value; no cap tested |
| Quick-action lane, about 3 a week | Mechanism supported; number not addressed | One-tap, low-effort actions get completed; no frequency evidence for task reminders |
| Weekly review with keep / snooze / drop | Indirect support only | Progress monitoring helps when outcomes are recorded; weekly digests and these buttons are untested |
| Routine-derived send windows | Partly supported, partly contradicted | Routine is good for exclusions; early morning is the worst slot; lunch and after-work are not shown to differ; routine-anchored timing never tested |
| Urgency inferred from the person's words | Not addressed | No study found |
| Piggybacking while the user is chatting | Supported by analogy; untested for chatbots | Phone-in-hand moments give faster and somewhat more frequent responses; mid-task interruptions feel worst |
| Backing off when ignored | Principle supported; thresholds untested | Dose-decay evidence; non-response treated as non-receptivity in the just-in-time framework; no trial of any back-off rule |
| Combined volume | Direction contradicted | Worst case rises from 7 a week plus deadlines to 11 or more; nothing in the evidence argues for more |

Several rows need more than a cell. On deadlines, trials comparing lead times of 24, 48 and 72 hours found no significant differences ([Guy et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22091980/)), so the proposal's instinct to time to the deadline is fine but no specific lead is better supported than another; the pattern from multi-reminder trials is one message early enough to prepare and one near the point of action. On opportunity nudges, the framework that defines just-in-time interventions separates "is this worth raising now" from "can the person take it in now" treats sending nothing as a legitimate choice, and warns that support delivered when the person is not receptive can harm engagement and cause "intervention fatigue" ([Nahum-Shani et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5364076)); that is a design vocabulary, not an effect estimate. A dry weekend or a sale answers the first question and leaves the second open.

On send windows, moving away from "the first allowed minute" is supported in direction if that minute falls soon after waking, since early morning is the one slot rated lowest wherever it was measured. The same finding counts against the proposed "before work" window. The lunch and after-work windows are reasonable, unproven choices. On piggybacking, the analogy is to notifications sent just after a call or text ended, which were accepted in a third to a half of the time; the chatbot version has not been studied, and notifications arriving mid-task are noticed fastest but rated most disruptive ([Mehrotra et al. 2016](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1502364/1/CHI16.pdf)). Delivery after the exchange ends fits the evidence; delivery in the middle of it does not.

On the two live nudges: both acted on, at a moment the user could act, matches the most common reason people give for clicking a notification. It carries almost no statistical weight. Two successes out of two would occur one time in four even if the true act rate were 50% (my arithmetic). Early nudges also benefit from novelty, which in HeartSteps made the starting effect four to five times the six-week average. And in the one mobile study that checked, people's ratings of timing did not track their behaviour. Record the result and keep counting.

## Eight changes, ranked by evidence and payoff

**First, replace the additive lane caps with one shared weekly budget.** Opportunity and quick-action nudges and the weekly review should draw from a single budget set near what the app sends today (a few a week), with "1 a day" kept as a ceiling that is rarely reached. Deadlines stay outside the budget. This follows the dose pattern in the trials, the platform experiments, and the per-user weekly budgets Pinterest describes ([Pinterest Engineering](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/user-state-based-notification-volume-optimization-7764118f73ff)). The budget's starting value is a judgement; the evidence supplies the direction, not the number.

**Second, gate every nudge on "can act now", and make "Let's do it" land on the prepared action.** Content and effort outweigh clock time, and the prospective-memory work says the cue should arrive when action is seconds away. A quick-action nudge should carry the order link, the drafted message or the pre-filled slot. An opportunity nudge should fire when the outside event and an allowed window coincide. If the binding obstacle is something else (the user no longer wants it, or does not know the first step), a link will not help; "Next steps" and "Not interested" exist for those cases.

**Third, write the back-off as explicit rules.** The framework's own example withholds follow-up after an ignored prompt, and the HeartSteps adaptive algorithm took about six days at five decisions a day to reduce sending to a participant whose responsiveness was falling ([Liao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.03539)). A learner will be slower still at a few nudges a week. A workable rule set: an item nudged twice with no response moves to the weekly review only; a run of ignored nudges across items cuts the weekly budget until the user re-engages. The thresholds are untested anywhere and are this report's suggestion.

**Fourth, use the routine to exclude, and demote the before-work window.** Keep sleep, work hours and the early morning out. Default to lunch and after-work on workdays and late morning onward on days off. Reserve before-work for items that must happen that morning. Among eligible windows, choose with the randomised scheme described in the next section.

**Fifth, cap each deadline at two touches.** One early enough to prepare and one near the point of action, naming the user's specific item and, where real, the cost of missing it. Stating the cost of a missed appointment cut no-shows from 11.1% to 8.4% in one hospital trial (recalled) ([Hallsworth et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0137306)). Beyond two reminders there is no evidence of added benefit.

**Sixth, piggyback after the exchange ends, one item at a time, with a fallback.** Prefer an item related to what was just discussed, count it against the budget, and send in a normal window if no conversation happens within a set time; the authors of the largest receptivity study recommend such a timeout for any policy that waits for a good moment ([Pielot et al. 2017](https://minoskt.github.io/papers/UbiComp17_Engagement.pdf)).

**Seventh, build the weekly review as a record of decisions.** Each item shows its status and takes one tap; the recorded outcome is the ingredient with support, and a small trial found monitoring raised goal attainment where reflection alone did not ([TU Delft 2025](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:4cf22026-32ed-4a51-bd36-5aebf3c6e232)). Offer short snooze horizons (later today, tomorrow morning) alongside next week or a landmark date. Make "drop" neutral and final. Keep the list short. If the review goes unopened for several weeks, shrink it; nothing shows people read weekly digests.

**Eighth, treat wording and inferred urgency as cheap defaults to verify.** Say "you mentioned", use possession framing, avoid "should" and "don't forget" (forceful language raised reactance at about r = .20 in a meta-analysis of message studies measuring attitudes, not behaviour) ([reactance meta-analysis](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/message-effects-on-psychological-reactance-meta-analyses/)), vary the phrasing between nudges, and phrase next steps as "when X, do Y" with a time and place. Since no evidence addresses inferring urgency from wording, confirm it with one tap at capture when the inference would change the lane, and log corrections.

## Learning from a few taps a week means counting, not modelling

Teams running adaptive prompts at five decisions a day concluded that one user's data is too thin to learn from alone; noise "can cripple the ability of an algorithm to learn when given access to only a single user's data" ([Tomkins et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09971)), and a trial with 140 decisions per person used a single shared model for that reason ([Trella et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02069)). At 3 to 10 nudges a week this app needs 14 to 47 weeks to reach 140. It has no other users to pool with. No source gives a minimum sample for a per-user learner to beat a fixed rule. The method has to have very few parameters, a prior, and patience.

Log one row per nudge: lane, item, window, workday or day off, piggybacked or cold, button pressed, minutes to the press, and whether the thing was done within a day and within a week. The app sees taps, not completions, so ask for the completion in the weekly review. Count "Let's do it" followed by completion as the success. Count "Later" as neutral and keep the time the user picks, since that is a free statement of when they expect to be able to act, to be tested against what happens at that time. Count "Not interested" and silence separately; the first is about the item, the second is the timing and fatigue signal. Weight behaviour over opinions about timing.

Keep one running success tally per window (three or four windows, no more) and choose among eligible windows in proportion to how well each has done, with every window guaranteed a minimum share so none is abandoned on early bad luck. This is the simplest form of the Thompson sampling these research groups use, and the guaranteed share copies HeartSteps, which bounded its send probability so the algorithm "will continue to explore and learn, instead of locking itself into a particular policy". Start each tally with a prior worth a handful of imaginary observations at the user's overall success rate; the literature supports using a prior and says nothing about what a good one is here. Do not add weather, lane or weekday as model features. At five nudges a week over three windows, each window collects about 20 observations in three months, and at 20 observations a 60% success rate carries a margin of about 21 points either way (my arithmetic). Differences smaller than that are noise. Review the tallies quarterly.

Handle fatigue outside the tally. Track nudges sent in the trailing seven days and compare success in heavier and lighter weeks, because effects decay with recent dose and a plain tally reacts late. Let old observations count for less over time. Change one thing at a time, and judge any change in volume over months, since the one long-running platform experiment found, Meta's, reported that the short-run result pointed the wrong way. The mute button, a rising share of "Not interested", and lengthening silences are the guardrails; any of them worsening after a change means reverting it.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the timing question. For a few messages a week about things the person chose, the gains available from picking the right hour are small and mostly about speed of reply, while the gains from sending only when the action is possible, making it one tap, and staying quiet otherwise are the ones the larger trials support. The proposed lanes get the kinds of nudge right. The risk in the proposal is arithmetic: three lanes with separate caps add up to more messages than today, on numbers nobody has tested.

The gap in the literature is also the opportunity. Nobody has measured how people respond over months to reminders of their own wishes with accept, defer and decline buttons, so this app's log will be the first relevant evidence for its own user. A year of honest tallies will say more about this person's good moments than any study cited here, provided the system keeps trying the alternatives often enough to find out.
